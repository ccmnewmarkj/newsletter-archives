Staying safe as AAPI journalists 

In an effort to address the growing safety concerns facing Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) community journalists, CCM’s Asian Media Initiative is launching its inaugural Safety Cohort. The program aims to equip vulnerable immigrant journalists serving AAPI communities with essential resources and expertise to navigate increasingly complex threats to their press freedom and safety, and is led by initiative director Kavitha Rajagopalan. The program is a collaboration with Newmark J-School’s Journalism Protection Initiative (JPI). 

"Just as Asian immigrant communities are facing escalating threats from travel bans, changing protections, and new policies around political speech, so too are the immigrant journalists who serve them," Rajagopalan said. "This program is the first effort to bring expert resources and guidance in press freedom and journalism safety to diverse teams in AAPI community media, who have often lacked resources to implement critical safety measures to protect their teams."

The program has selected 13 newsroom teams that cover communities in seven different states and represent a diversity of AAPI communities. These outlets publish in English, Arabic, Bahasa Indonesia, Bangla, Chinese (Mandarin), Farsi, Hindi, Kannada, Marathi, and Telugu. Notably, more than half of the participating newsrooms operate with fewer than five staff members, highlighting the resource constraints these vital information providers face.

The cohort is also composed of a diverse range of media organizations, including three newsrooms that focus on documenting human rights abuses affecting displaced communities - and two of these newsrooms are led by journalists in exile from their home countries. Four special-focus outlets report on AAPI communities through specific lenses such as climate change, disinformation, or immigration. The remaining six are local community news outlets serving Arab American, Bangladeshi American, Chinese American, Indian American, and Indonesian American communities in the U.S.

The program structure begins with a workshop led by Newmark J-School's in-house safety advisor Yemile Bucay, where teams will undergo risk assessments and then be matched with a specialized safety consultant who will help design and implement a tailored safety intervention and curated resources.  

JPI Director Joel Simon said “effective safety protocols begin with a risk assessment, which can help journalists and news organizations understand their vulnerabilities and prioritize their responses. The good news is that there are effective mitigation strategies and a wealth of resources to draw on once risks are identified.” The initiative has developed a matrix and toolkit to aid newsrooms and journalists, which will be shared publicly in the near future. 

Prior to joining the program, participating newsrooms ranked their concerns on a scale of 1-5 across several key areas: the impact of changing U.S. immigration policy on staff (including travel bans and ICE enforcement); surveillance by U.S. or overseas governments; digital harassment and threats of violence (including doxxing); legal or financial threats to editorial independence from within their communities; and direct repression from overseas governments, such as harassment of family members or influence efforts.

These initial rankings will be compared with post-workshop assessments to evaluate changes in perceived risks and measure improvements in awareness and preparedness for responding to threats. 

Throughout the program, participants will have access to consultations with press freedom and First Amendment experts, immigration attorneys, and journalist safety specialists, creating a holistic support system for these essential yet vulnerable media professionals.

This groundbreaking initiative represents a critical step forward in protecting the vital role that immigrant journalists play in keeping their communities informed, connected, and empowered in an increasingly complex media landscape.
