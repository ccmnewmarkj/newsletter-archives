Publishers weigh in on challenges and successful strategies

Last week we held an important event on the state of community media with four New York-area publishers: Henry J. Levy from Jewish Post, Kishor Panthi from Khasokhas, Debralee Santos from The Manhattan Times and The Bronx Free Press, and C. Zawadi Morris from BK Reader. The event was organized by CCM’s Director of Communications Jehangir Khattak and moderated by Newmark J-School’s MA in Engagement Journalism program Director Carla Zanoni, who oversees CCM. If you missed the live event, you can watch the recording. 

These successful publishers addressed the urgent sustainability challenges facing community media outlets and shared innovative strategies for generating revenue while still serving their communities in the ways they need. 

Among the most pressing challenges the panelists shared were restrictions on government agency advertising. Despite a 2021 law that mandates New York City agencies to advertise in community media outlets, many outlets like BK Reader and Khasokas have experienced a significant drop in ad buys from these city agencies recently.

Additionally, Panthi said, for Nepali-language publishers like himself, the high costs of new technology are a major challenge for non-English community media because most AI tools don't support their language. 

A big challenge for Santos, whose publications serve a predominantly Latino and immigrant audience, is how to combat misinformation and disinformation. As hyperlocal journalists deeply embedded in their communities, her team witnesses firsthand how rapidly false information spreads. “The head on a swivel approach is not very conducive to good, valuable reporting,” she explained, adding that allowing misinformation to proliferate within small communities even for an afternoon can undo much of the work of sharing accurate information. 

For BK Reader, audience development and retention remain the greatest challenge. Morris shared that the first few years of operation BK Reader’s unique visitor rate was approximately 85% and return visitor rate was about 15%, which was competitive across the industry. However, she has seen little growth in loyalty. “Fast forward 12 years and it's still around that rate… I would love to be able to see a little bit more retention in my audience,” she said. 

For the Jewish Post, print was always king but Levy said seeing even established legacy newspapers like the Star-Ledger in New Jersey shutting down print operations “can be disheartening.” He now feels growing pressure to pivot to a digital first strategy. 

However, not all hope is lost as the publishers shared their advice on attracting advertisers and diversifying revenue streams. Levy said that he has learned over the years to collaborate with potential advertisers, usually local businesses in, or catering to, his community, rather than just selling to them. Levy said he offers to have his team help with digital strategy as a way of showing that he wants both businesses to succeed. “Once you can get into the person's mind in that manner you're a member of his team, you're not just a salesman,” Levy noted.

Santos also suggested partnering with institutions and businesses that also need more engagement so there is a common goal and each can offer the other “a fresh set of eyes” on strategy. “We need to ask ourselves what we're partnering for and in service to…because we are a business as well,” Santos said. 

Panthi also explained his four separate revenue streams for Khasokas: advertisements from local businesses and government agencies, Infolinks because Google Ads does not support the Nepali language, fundraising, and philanthropic funding. He explained that his outlet attempted a subscription method but for small communities like his, it was not a successful revenue generator. 

For BK Reader she noted that sponsored content has recently produced nearly triple the advertising revenue. “Every publication will have to come up with a different formula based off their audience. All bets are off in terms of what we used to think about the revenue model for newspapers…you really have to get creative,” Morris added.

Policy Updates 

We are keeping an eagle eye on legislation around the country that will have an impact on the community media landscape. 

We highlighted this news item in last week’s newsletter regarding Maryland. The state General Assembly introduced HB 51, which would require news outlets to give 120-day notice to communities, workers, and the Labor Department before that news outlet would be sold. Both Rebuild Local News and the News Guild support the bill, noting it could prevent private equity firms from acquiring and reducing local news coverage. 

In last month’s newsletter we discussed New York State Senate Bill S1865, called the Lift Our Communities, Advertise Locally (LOCAL) Act. The proposal would incentivize small businesses to place advertisements in community media. 

Several other states including New York are debating on how best to provide various revenue streams and friendlier environments for local and community media outlets. Journalist and advisor to our colleagues at Rebuild Local News, Matt Pearce, shared his analysis in his newsletter. 

Up for debate at the moment: 

Washington SB 5400 - Would create a Local News Journalism Corps Program and grants up to $15,000 for journalists covering civic affairs in underserved communities

New Mexico SB 110 and SB 111 - Proposal would give $4 million a year through 2030 in employment tax credits for the hiring of local journalists and print press workers 

Illinois SB 1732 - Bill would require Google and Meta to pay news outlets for using those outlets’ content in search results and news curation 

Oregon SB 686 - Similar to the Illinois SB 1732 proposal, but adds a provision to allow tech companies to donate to a university-run news consortium instead of paying fees to news outlets 

New York S4401 - Very similar to Illinois SB 1732 and Oregon SB 686 regarding fees paid to journalism outlets 

Hawaii HB 1458 - Proposes to put a tax on social media advertising and use the revenue to fund broadband internet access across the state and hyperlocal journalism initiatives
