A groundbreaking report about AAPI Media and learning more about AI and visual journalism

Our Asian Media Initiative has had a busy six months! In December, Director Kavitha Rajagopalan, with the help of Research and Development Associate Jennifer Cheng and others, published the first-ever map and directory of AAPI community-serving media. These resources document, categorize, and map nearly 700 outlets serving a variety of Asian American and diaspora communities. The directory and interactive map provide a crucial resource to journalists and researchers in understanding the impact of AAPI community media and the communities themselves in a way that has not been done before. 

On May 26, the 100th anniversary of the 1924 Immigration Act (Johnson-Reed Act) that banned all immigration from Asia, Rajagopalan published a comprehensive report AAPI News Media: Origins and Futures. 

This report explores how diverse AAPI communities – from San Francisco’s Chinatown to New York City’s Little Syria – established and lost their news media sectors during the 19th and early 20th centuries, only to re-establish a vibrant news media ecosystem the AMI documented in the directory and map. 

But, it is much more than just a timeline of milestones. The report also discusses the rise of digital disinformation and its harmful effects on everything from the pandemic to elections, online harassment, overseas influence, and other pervasive threats facing AAPI media, as well as the need to keep working at making these outlets, which serve the fastest-growing demographic in the U.S., more sustainable, safe, and reliable. 

“With this wide-angle view of the entire AAPI news media ecosystem through its long history, and in all its forms, we hope to build awareness of this powerful, often overlooked, mechanism for engaging AAPI voters and supporting AAPI communities,” Rajagopalan said. 

For more information about the report and the Asian Media Initiative, please email Kavitha.Rajagopalan@journalism.cuny.edu.

AI power is in the eye of the beholder

A few weeks ago, we were lucky enough to have John Smock, Newmark J-School’s Director of Photojournalism, and Paris Brown, Associate Publisher of the Baltimore Banner, join us for a virtual event on artificial intelligence and visuals. You can watch the recording of the event here. 

AI has been in the news nonstop this past year as the number of ways it can be used seems to grow leaps and bounds by the day, with no signs of slowing down. Journalism has been grappling with how best to use generative AI in a variety of ways. CCM held an event with Aimee Rinehart, Senior Product Manager of AI Strategy for The Associated Press, last October on how community media can harness the power of AI as well. 

As Rinehart explained, generative AI is similar to a “neural network” like those in our brains that help us make connections, learn from mistakes, and find meaning beyond the literal. While generative AI is similar, she was quick to say it is not a duplicate or replacement for those human skills. 

Smock and Brown expanded on how that works with visual journalism - everything from photography, infographics, artwork, and interactive features. Smock noted that the “biggest challenge for journalists and visual AI is fear of the unknown…and hype.”

Any new technology can be intimidating at first, but particularly with AI, Smock said “there is so much almost mystical language around AI that anthropomorphizes it…people are freaked out by it.” However, he also noted that the potential to add value to a community media outlet’s audience is too great to be scared to test the waters. 

As Brown explained, being a small newsroom is not a disadvantage when trying out a new technology - it’s an asset. The “agility” of a small operation allows newsrooms like the Baltimore Banner to continuously experiment with AI. 

Her newsroom has employed AI to cover staffing gaps for administrative tasks like grammar checks, transcribing audio and video recordings of interviews to text, and even creating timelines for larger projects. They also use it to create culturally-specific stock images of people of color in certain professions or in certain scenarios for their stories, a gap in readily available stock images online. 

Smock noted that with this great potential to help newsrooms have time and energy to build trust with and expand their audiences, there is an equal opportunity for misinformation, disinformation, and fake content. And, with an election coming up, there is fertile ground for this kind of content to have disastrous effects.

“Some camera manufacturers are working to watermark original images in the camera. Other orgs are working on metadata that would create an indelible breadcrumb trail that would show if images are original, manipulated or created by AI,” he said. 

However, the most important thing is to remember that AI is just another tool in a journalist’s arsenal, not a replacement for a skill. He noted: “old-fashioned journalistic sleuthing can do a lot to confirm or deny the authenticity of most images: how credible is the source? Are there other images of the topic from other sources? Does what I am looking at make sense? In the end, our credibility is all we have.”

Workshop on juvenile justice reporting 

Newmark J-School is thrilled to announce a call for applications for “Restorative v. Punitive: Reporting on Juvenile Justice at a Crossroads” — a free two-day, online workshop funded by The Tow Foundation and aimed at journalists covering juvenile justice, criminal justice and related beats. Space is limited for the training sessions, to be run June 24-25. Fill out this short form and include a proposal for a juvenile justice story you’d like to work on. Qualified journalists will be accepted as they apply. 

The goal: to help prepare you to tackle a national juvenile justice landscape where uncertainty, political divisions and high stakes demand strong reporting that helps deliver insight and action.

If you’re accepted, you’ll work with juvenile justice experts, lawmakers, formerly justice-involved youth and top-notch journalists at a time when the movement to emphasize rehabilitation over punishment is playing out unevenly amid battles over reform efforts. You’ll also get feedback on your pitches from veteran editors. Deadline is rolling.
