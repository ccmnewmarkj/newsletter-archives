From Tragedy to Action: Community Media's Role in Covering the Missing

This image is from the 15th and final story of the Crosswinds News’ MMIWG2T Historical Research and Reporting Project series

Over the last month, the tragic disappearance of Nancy Guthrie, the 84-year-old mother of TV news anchor Savannah Guthrie, has been a prominent topic in national news cycles. While the ongoing 33-day search initially received massive media attention and resources, the coverage has naturally started to fade. Watching this cycle play out begs the question: what happens when the national media moves on to the next story? 

The coverage also sparked a conversation among us at the Center for Community Media, in which we discussed that this kind of breathless coverage is rarely accorded to all missing women and girls. We asked ourselves: Who gets to be a national priority? Who is left to vanish in silence? And, when a victim’s life circumstances are different, what are the ways in which community media fills the voids? 

So this week, we bring examples of Black and Native community media across the country who speak up in moments like these, who show up for long-haul reporting, and who craft ways of doing better reporting together. ​​ 

Naming the contrast and demanding better 

In a searing editorial, The Washington Informer confronted the stark disparity in who receives our nation's collective anxiety and empathy. Given the persistent and dominating coverage of the disappearance of Nancy Guthrie, The Informer explicitly named the glaring contrast for Black families. "Wall-to-wall coverage, push alerts, and breathless updates appear almost instantly when there is a missing person linked to fame, privilege, or proximity to power," the editorial board wrote. "Yet, when Black people — especially children — go missing, the silence is often deafening." By pointing out that this media disparity "reinforces a hierarchy of empathy," The Informer reframed the issue: it is not just about a lack of coverage; it is felt as a "cruel, unspoken message" that some lives are inherently less newsworthy. The Informer used the moment not to diminish one family's genuine pain, but to demand that the standard of exhaustive pursuit be applied equally to all missing persons.

While The Informer called for an equal expansion of the media’s spotlight, Word In Black offered a contrasting yet equally essential vision of what “better” coverage must look like: a demand for reflection and restraint. In a powerful piece authored by Dr. Frances Murphy Draper, Publisher, and CEO of The AFRO-American Newspapers, and the chair of its board, representing the fourth generation of her family to lead the historic publication founded in 1892, she warned against the cultural habit shaped by television and podcasts that trains audiences to expect neatly resolved narratives. "Real life does not owe us clarity on a schedule," wrote Dr. Draper. "It does not reveal motives neatly or reward close reading with certainty. Sometimes it asks us to bear witness without filling in the blanks." By insisting that "restraint is a moral discipline," she reminds us that "people are not stories, and that tragedy is not a teaching tool," urging a public response rooted in patience and compassion rather than speculation and commentary.

Building for the long haul 

Community media also builds its coverage for sustained attention when other media outlets have moved on to the next big story, creating mechanisms that can lead to accountability for injustice.  

The Kansas City Defender is a case in point. Rejecting the traditional, detached model of journalism, the Black-led publication has merged community organizing with reporting. The commitment was already noticeably visible in September 2022, when community activist Bishop Tony Caldwell posted a desperate video warning that Black women were disappearing from local neighborhoods—stating, "We got a serial killer again. And ain't nobody saying nothing. The media’s not covering it."—The Defender immediately amplified the alarm. The local police department explicitly dismissed the claims, issuing statements that the reports were "completely unfounded rumors," a denial that mainstream outlets repeated.

But The Defender refused to accept the official line. Its founder and publisher, Ryan Sorrell, explained in an interview with Capital B News he didn't retract the story; instead, he fortified the community's platform. "We didn’t apologize. We actually doubled down on our commitment to the community, and I published an open letter that said we’re not backing down, Black Kansas Citians, we have your back," Sorrell stated, in reference to the outlet utilizing their digital platforms and podcasting to center the voices and warnings of Black women. Tragically, their reporting was validated weeks later when a young Black woman escaped from a captor's basement. The Defender didn’t just cover the story; they created the platform necessary to validate what their community knew was real, and forced an investigation when others looked away.

Protecting the search through independent press

“And everywhere you would turn, you would see the story about the non-Native girl that was missing. But we had to fight and really get the message out: Hey, we care about our Native girl as well.”

This frustration, voiced by Amber Davis, a member of the Muscogee Nation who serves on the board of directors for the Missing and Murdered Indigenous People (MMIP) Central Oklahoma Chapter, permeates throughout Indigenous communities nationwide, where the search for missing individuals is made even harder by mainstream media silence. That’s where Crosswinds News steps in. Located on the Muscogee Nation Reservation, the Native-owned, Native-staffed newsroom is one of a few independently operated Native American local media outlets in the country and now covers stories from and for Oklahoma's 39 tribes. Because they are fully independent, rather than a communications department functioning under a specific tribal government, they are uniquely positioned to report on the realities of Indigenous life without political oversight.

In their feature article, "Missing and Murdered, Yet Underreported," Crosswinds provided an astute look at the specific roadblocks Native families face when a loved one disappears. They anchored their investigation in the devastating reality of Britney Tiger, who went missing and was found dead in 2018. When her case was largely ignored by the wider public, it was Crosswinds who explored more. Their reporting revealed that one or more individuals had dragged her body across a wooded area, yet her death was ruled "Undetermined" and remains unsolved today.

This persistent reporting underscores why their independent status is critical. Because Crosswinds doesn't answer to tribal politicians, as many tribally funded media do, they are empowered to challenge a dangerous dynamic described, in the piece, by Troy Littledeer, a Keetoowah Cherokee journalist and freelancer for Osage News. Littledeer noted a frequent preference for tribal leadership to control the narrative, explaining the real-world cost: “It becomes safer to their egos or whatever to publish 'good news only,'” Littledeer stated. “Which means corruption, neglect, and abuse stay buried. But then the victims lose the most, especially in crises like MMIP...” By insisting that a free, uncensored press is a necessary survival tool for the community, Crosswinds ensures the MMIP crisis is documented with the absolute honesty it requires.

Building a national campaign

Community media outlets aren't just covering these stories individually; they are joining forces to ensure missing persons stay in the public eye long after the initial disappearance.

This shared approach is at the heart of the "Missing & Black 2025" campaign, a nationwide initiative highlighted by the Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder and launched by the National Newspaper Publishers Association (NNPA). Founded in 1940, the NNPA represents a powerful network of more than 200 Black-owned community newspapers across the United States. Rather than waiting for the broader media landscape to shift, Black Press USA is mobilizing its own extensive network to create a sustained drumbeat for missing Black individuals. The campaign isn't built around a single investigative report; instead, it uses the NNPA World News App and the reach of local member papers to make reporting on missing persons a daily, routine practice.

The Spokesman-Recorder featured the fierce self-reliance driving this effort. They elevated the perspective of Carolyn Davis, the campaign's architect, who accentuated the power of Black Press USA to take ownership of the narrative. “We need to use our own networks and resources like the NNPA to keep these missing people in the public eye so they are not forgotten,” Davis stated.

The outlet also highlighted the vital partnerships that make this work possible, elevating the voice of Natalie Wilson, co-founder of the Black and Missing Foundation, Inc. Wilson emphasized that this initiative is created to help journalists remove unintentional bias and establish a fairer, more consistent approach to covering the cases. Through amplifying this nationwide campaign, the Spokesman-Recorder showcased exactly how community publishers are actively working together to build a more impartial and responsible media landscape.

Emerging News

Eyes on Iran: Perspectives from the Diaspora Press

In this week’s Spotlight, we are closely watching the unfolding events in Iran. During moments of international crisis, diaspora media provides important context and historical memory that frequently goes missing from daily mainstream headlines. For those looking to gain a better understanding of the situation through the lens of the Iranian American community, we want to share a few crucial voices and resources:

Tehran Magazine recently published, before the attack on Iran started, "The Voice They Tried to Silence," a piece that powerfully showcases the Iranian diaspora's pervasive presence and deep connection to the homeland's politics.

Iranshahr News Agency offers a vital historical perspective, illuminating the fact that a large segment of the Iranian American population was exiled during the Islamic Revolution and remains loyal to the legacy of the Shah.

Dispatches from an Iranian American Expat, a Substack by David Shams, provides a remarkably smart and thoughtful perspective in his Substack piece, "Beyond the Bombs: Why Iran’s Struggle for Self-Determination Predates Operation Epic Fury." He expertly breaks down how the Iranian fight for self-determination long predates this latest crisis.

Guarded Domains is a Substack by Reza H. Akbari, providing rich, ongoing sociopolitical analysis of Iranian history, culture, and society.
