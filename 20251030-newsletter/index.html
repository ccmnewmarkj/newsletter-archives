 When Home-Country News Hits Home: How Khasokhas Covered the Protests in Nepal and the Aftereffects on the Diaspora


Credit: Himal Subedi, Source: Wikimedia Commons. Nepal protests agaisnt corruption, September 9, 2025

Welcome to the Center for Community Media’s new Spotlight on Community Media newsletter! Once a month, you will see a Q+A, interview, case study, or other feature on a community media publisher or issue relevant to community-serving media outlets and those who support them. Our goal for this newsletter is to tell stories about the people who make up the community media ecosystem, how they work, their successes and lessons learned. Do you have a publisher or issue you would like to see featured? Email Mythili Sampathkumar at mythili.sampathkumar@journalism.cuny.edu.

When protests erupted in Nepal last month, largely organized by the country’s youth as an answer to government corruption, the story rippled far beyond Kathmandu. For Kishor Panthi, publisher of the Nepali-language Khasokhas and English-language NEPYork, the crisis reminded him of covering the 2015 earthquake for the Nepali diaspora – those far from their families and invested in the news from home. He knew that the protests, especially once they turned violent, demanded careful, bilingual, cross-border reporting that could reach diaspora audiences in NYC and across the U.S.

Panthi recalled how his newsroom covered what he called the “two parts of the protests.” The first day, he said, was “a very peaceful protest that most of us thought was overdue.” By the second day however, “the anger [of a generation] became an excuse for rioting,” he explained. The violence left 22 people dead and hundreds injured. The prime minister resigned, and young people took to the Discord app to organize voting for a new leader. 

Panthi said their small team in Kathmandu – composed of a few freelance reporters – got caught in the violence. One was hit in the face with a rubber bullet and had to be hospitalized for days. The others carried on, allowing Panthi’s newsrooms to bring accurate news to his American audience.

What followed would be a wide range of stories for the newsrooms covering the aftermath of the roughly weeklong protests and how the continued civil unrest was affecting the Nepalese diaspora community in the U.S. Thus far Khasokhas and NEPYork have done stories on the collapse of Nepali tourism and the U.S. State Department’s travel warnings, how the second-generation and American-born Nepalis are viewing the protests, GoFundMe campaigns started by Nepalis and the new government itself asking the diaspora for help in the wake of vandalism and rioting, as well as a slew of reports on immigration. 

The newsrooms have also addressed the rampant misinformation making the rounds in the diaspora, some of it AI-generated. “My mom saw videos on TikTok [of politicians’ speeches] and believed them – I had to tell her they were fake,” he said. His mother happened to be visiting the US when the unrest erupted, and he saw what she was watching. He was able to debunk the ‘news’ in the videos by cross-referencing them with reputable news reports from Kathmandu. 

Panthi said the biggest challenge in reporting on the protests back in Nepal has been combating misinformation. His reporters went where misinformation tends to take hold: they did reports on Instagram reels, TikTok, and in WhatsApp groups, including a series of videos combating deep fake videos and inaccurate reports in Nepali, to target populations who may not turn to English-language sources for verification. 

The unrest comes at an already anxious moment for the Nepalese community given President Trump’s heightened rhetoric around immigration and a tightening of policy and enforcement. Around 15,000 Nepalese came here during the upheaval of the 2015 earthquake and now many of them may have to return during a moment of political violence and uncertainty. According to Panthi, several thousand Nepalis in the U.S. remain on this Temporary Protected Status (TPS), a temporary immigration status for those whose home country is facing violence or a natural disaster. Readers have reached out and “are desperate for news about what is going on in Nepal and with the U.S. government,” he said.

He tries to help quell the high levels of anxiety with information and resources. “You have to report the on-the-ground reality both [there and here]…you have to know what’s really happening,” Panthi said.

He also said mainstream media outlets may not understand how to report on events for diaspora audiences since they “apply the same script” for protests and political violence rather than understanding the nuances. For instance, Panthi mentioned that some mainstream English-language media outlets tried to tell the story about Nepali youth and equate it with the story of the Bangladeshi uprising earlier this year. According to the publisher, those stories did not take into account the distinct histories, forms of government, socioeconomic issues, cultures, majority religions, and regional relationships – all of which played key roles in the uprisings. 

“Some initially romanticized violence and arson, describing it as spontaneous rage,” Panthi explained. “But evidence suggested otherwise. Additionally, some media initially described the original protest as being only because of a social media ban. However, the main causes of frustration were poverty, unemployment, corruption, and political instability,” he said. 

Shared approaches among diaspora newsrooms

Panthi’s challenges mirror strategies other community media use when “home” is thousands of miles away, and political or social tensions rise.

At The Haitian Times, editors have long covered political turmoil, gang violence, and natural disasters in Haiti, for Haitian-Americans. During the 2021 assassination of President Jovenel Moïse, the outlet focused on tracing how instability in Port-au-Prince shaped local fears in Brooklyn and Miami.

Similarly, India Currents has helped Indian-Americans parse misinformation circulating on WhatsApp and Facebook. This became particularly important during COVID-19 surges and during recent periods of farmers’ protests. The magazine uses bilingual explainers and reader Q&As to verify viral claims and give readers context about what’s true and what’s exaggerated. 

The Dominican outlet Diario Libre launched DLUSA, a digital product for Dominican-Americans. When protests erupted in the Dominican Republic over election delays in 2020, DLUSA tailored coverage for U.S.-based readers by explaining political stakes, sharing on-the-ground video from its reporters, and contextualizing it for diaspora audiences in New York and Florida. 

For many in the U.S., community media are not considered  a go-to for foreign affairs coverage. But as Khasokhas and others’ experience shows, they ought to be. These outlets bring nuanced, accurate, and meaningful reporting that matters to their audience – and us all. Moreover, in an era of increasingly deceptive misinformation and disinformation campaigns, there are media literacy lessons to be learned from immigrant news sources as well.
