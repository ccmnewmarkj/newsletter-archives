Meet our new Executive Director Mikhael Simmonds
  
CCM Executive Director Mikhael Simmonds
We are thrilled to welcome Mikhael Simmonds to the helm of the Center for Community Media! Before joining us, Simmonds served as the director of new relationships at the Solutions Journalism Network, where he co-led operations, strategy, and execution around how SJN trained journalists, developed teaching products and coached news outlets. As a regional manager, he co-developed and managed solutions journalism projects with news partners in Kenya, Nigeria, and the U.S. We sat down for a chat with him during his first week.

CCM: What made you want to come work for the Center for Community Media (besides working with some lovely people who deeply care about journalism serving communities)?

Mikhael Simmonds: So I've been working in international affairs and journalism for well, a decade though. Hmm. Well, I want to sit with that for a second! I got my start in community media. One of my earlier jobs was at the Amsterdam News. I later moved on to Solutions Journalism [Network]. And there it was very much international, but also based in New York. So my interest in CCM really came from that duality – people who have multiple identities, multiple nationalities, multiple cultures living next to each other and telling relevant news for their audiences – and having conversations with each other, among each other. So when the opportunity to work with CCM came up, I was like, ‘oh, you're speaking my language!’

CCM: That’s right, you are a CUNY alum and our team does also speak multiple languages.

MS: I am an immigrant myself [from Trinidad]. I went to undergrad at CUNY, grad school at CUNY. My undergrad was international affairs, with a bunch of different immigrants at City College. And we all talked about the multiple identities, multiple spaces issue. Now there’s a job where I get to work deeply in that.

CCM: What was your exposure to community media before working at Amsterdam News?

MS: So my exposure early on was indirect from going to City College. I didn't have direct exposure in the sense of I was not a regular reader or regular listener. But it did come through when you go to school with people who are regular readers and regular listeners. Only when I really started to dive a little deeper into journalism and not the international affairs part, did I start to come across or engage more deeply with community media in different ways. And by community media, I don't just mean New York community media. I was able to engage more broadly while at SJN, working with West Coast partners, and on the East Coast some of the Philadelphia newsrooms like WURD, which is one of the only Black stations and FunTimes Magazine, an African community magazine in Philadelphia. So that's where I really started to get into the media part of community media overall.

CCM: Given your background, what do you think is the intersection between international affairs and community-centered journalism?

MS: You know, it’s funny how we view the world is partially dependent on your identity. So I started like that saying ‘it's an intersection if you're looking in – but if you're living it, it's your reality. It's your full totality.’ So for example, if you are an Asian, African, Latin American immigrant, the idea of international affairs is embedded in the fact that you are living in, say, New York or San Francisco and your family is spread across the world. Any policy, any type of conversation, any type of racism or violence, whether you are a recipient of discrimination or a perpetrator of it – that is just the reality you live in as someone who is part of a “non-mainstream” community. So, it's not really an intersection. It's just life.

CCM: Does this translate to when people immigrated as well and in what media conversations they can be involved?

MS: Even when you start thinking about different waves of immigration, like if you moved here in the last 20 years because you came here for college, for example, that's a very different socioeconomic background than somebody who moved here 40 years ago, and their kids who grew up here, grew up in a very different space than the parents. Even though there are differences between the waves, you start to have international affairs conversations. What policies are affecting you as a refugee? Did you come here for school? Are you discriminated against within your own home country? Do you have privilege in your own country and you can travel but other people can't? And the conversations here all come from that. When you think of a place like New York City, the conversation about international affairs is always “up here,” [holds hand up high] in the New York Times, United Nations, international organizations. But really, the majority of people who are closest to this issue are living in Queens, Brooklyn, Bronx, Harlem. Leaving them out of the conversation, out of the American conversation, quite frankly, could be racist and classist depending on the reason that people are locked out of that situation.

CCM: This sounds like a good place to talk about throwing more support towards BIPOC-run community media.

MS: You can have all these different ideas of journalism, but you can't do journalism well if we don't reflect the totality of lived experiences. And community media helps fill that or starts that conversation in many ways, or brings humanity to these topics. We need to continue supporting the people closest to an issue, because it helps give a full idea of life and because they're also closest to the solution as well.

CCM: What is one of the best examples of community media reporting you have seen?

MS:  The one that comes to mind is a piece from FunTimes Magazine, which I was fortunate enough to collaborate as well…about informal banking systems helping local West African communities become more financially stable. I love that because it’s a solutions story but also because it’s saying ‘hey, immigrant groups are not just victims of crimes, they are solving their own problems, just not in a western structure.’ It’s an illustration of a community bringing a solution with them from [their home country] and we can add to this melting pot of America as well.

News from Inside the Community Media Sector

 

Congratulations to Capital B/Capital B Atlanta for celebrating its first birthday and year of bringing news to the Black community in Atlanta and beyond.

Another big congratulations to LaSharah Bunting who has been named the new CEO and Executive Director of the Online News Association! Bunting was most recently a Vice President and Executive Editor at Simon & Schuster, where she acquired and edited nonfiction titles. She was previously Director of Journalism at the John S. and James L. Knight Foundation as well.

Three cheers for Helina Selemon, a professor at Newmark J-School, on being named New York Amsterdam News’ new science reporter for the Blacklight Investigative Unit! Selemon was previously with the Associated Press.

Round of applause to media strategist and nonprofit executive Solomon Lieberman, who has been hired as the new CEO and publisher of the Reader Institute for Community Journalism, which operates the Chicago Reader. Lieberman takes over from Tracy Baim, who was instrumental in collaborating with CCM and pushing for an executive order signed by Chicago Mayor Lori Lightfoot last year which committed the city to using at least half of its advertising budget in community media outlets.

The American Press Institute’s first Inclusion Index newsroom cohort members in Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh Tribune-Review, PublicSource, Pittsburgh City Paper and The Pitt News) shared their respective findings and strategic plans with community members following their participation in the program. The API Inclusion Index is a tool that can help newsrooms reckon with the systemic changes needed to address diversity disparities inside and out of their organizations through assessing seven key areas that could impact communities of color.

Mike Rispoli of Free Press, Elizabeth Green of Civic News Company (which publishes Chalkbeat and Votebeat), and Darryl Holliday of City Bureau collaborated to write a new report titled: "The Roadmap for Local News: An Emergent Approach to Meeting Civic Information Needs.” Joe Amditis of the Center for Cooperative Media wrote about how New Jersey “is a required stop on any roadmap for local news,” based on the report.

The Executive Program, News Innovation and Leadership at Newmark J-School writes about our Black Media Initiative’s Product Strategy training program and why more Black newsrooms are pursuing opportunities through various products to engage with their audiences. The BMI is led by Director Cheryl Thompson-Morton.

For those looking to explore collaborations, Community Info Coop has developed a database of philanthropic grantmaking from cities across the country to identify trends in DEI and giving in arts, media, tech, and speech issues.

Fellowships & Opportunities

Solutions Journalism Network, Trusting News, and Hearken are taking applications for their Advancing Democracy Fellowship. Twenty U.S. newsroom teams will be invited to learn about better ways to engage with their communities around elections and politics, reduce polarization, build or rebuild trust, and provide powerful solutions-focused coverage that can help people envision how to move forward. Deadline is February 14. 

Syracuse University’s Newhouse School of Public Communications is now accepting entries for the 2023 Mirror Awards, which honor the reporters, editors, and teams of writers who hold a mirror to their own industry for the public’s benefit. Deadline is February 15. 

New York Amsterdam News is accepting applications for its Beyond the Barrel of the Gun Fellowship. The fellow will be an early to midcareer journalist who will work with the investigative unit to report on the root causes and the impact of gun violence within Black and Brown communities, as well as the solutions to this plague. Deadline is February 17. 

The International Women's Media Foundation is accepting applications and nominations for three awards: The Anja Niedringhaus Courage in Photojournalism Award, which recognizes the importance of visual journalistic work that inspires us to take action and compels us to better understand the world. The Gwen Ifill Award is open to U.S. women and non-binary journalists of color working in the news media. Candidates for the award will be evaluated on criteria including their record of outstanding achievement in journalism, and the extent to which they represent the values Ifill embodied, including in the areas of mentorship, leadership, and commitment to diversity in journalism. The Courage in Journalism Awards honor the brave journalists who report on taboo topics, work in environments hostile to women, and share difficult truths. The deadline for all three is March 3. 

Type Media Center is accepting applications for the Ida B. Wells Fellowship. The one-year fellowship helps reporters complete their first substantial work of investigative reporting by providing a $25,000 award and intensive mentoring from dedicated editors at Type Investigations. Deadline is March 12. 

ProPublica is taking applications for its Investigative Editor Training Program, which is designed to help expand the ranks of editors with investigative experience in more newsrooms across the country, with a focus on people from underrepresented backgrounds. Deadline is March 13.

The New Jersey Civic Information Consortium has an open call for grant proposals. In 2023, the Consortium will focus on funding projects supporting the journalism pipeline, civic engagement, and election-related work. Grants up to $100,000 will be awarded from a $1 million pool. Deadline is March 31.

The Women's Media Center's Progressive Women's Voices 2023 is taking applications for its leadership and training program. Representing a range of expertise and diversity across race, class, geography, sexual orientation, ability, and generation, participants receive advanced, comprehensive training and tools to position themselves as media spokespeople in their fields, thereby changing the conversation on issues that fill headlines. Deadline is April 2.

Tips & Tools

The Open Notebook offers some writing advice about how to center science stories around unconventional main characters for a fresh take on your reporting. 

The Journalist’s Resource shares information on how to access and use the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse, housed at Syracuse University, for federal government data, especially if you need data which would have required you to fill out a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. 

Here is some research on the substitute teacher shortage and why there should be more coverage of it for your communities, also from The Journalist’s Resource. 

Big Local News, a project housed at Stanford, has partnered up with Datasette, a free and open-source tool for exploring data. The result is the ability for users to be able to run keyword searches, write custom SQL queries and export records to other formats.
