 A softball tournament to a festival, community media engages people in more personal ways

Instagram: @palaradionet

Community engagement is at the heart of any community media publisher and outlet’s work, but it is not always easy to engage the communities we serve – between, to name a few, the challenges of finding enough funding or the right type of funding for staff and other needs, a changing social media landscape, competition with digital-first and larger outlets, and access to broadband internet in poorer or rural communities. 

This is part of the reason why many community media publishers around the country have taken to non-traditional forms of engaging with their audiences in a more personal way and often offline. 

One such example is Pa' La Radio, an independent, family-owned digital radio station, based in Philadelphia's Harrowgate neighborhood, which recently hosted an annual softball tournament in Juniata Park. The station was relaunched as a creators hub for Spanish-proficient media professionals in the region. COO Lina Bonó noted it now serves a dual purpose for Latino Caribbean communities in the area - a place for family-oriented entertainment and a place to build a revenue stream for community organizations that serve the communities and that need financial support. 

The organizers decided on creating a softball tournament due to its cultural resonance with the communities: Philadelphia is 62% Puerto Rican, followed by 10% Dominican, two Caribbean communities that share the love for baseball. Vendors were brought in and proceeds and donations benefited the local Boys & Girls Club as well as relief efforts after an explosion in San Cristobal, Dominican Republic. 

As CCM’s Latino Media Initiative Director Jesenia De Moya Correa observed: “There were no talks about journalism or any interactions with folks trying to get their opinion on the news. Just chitchatting and playing ball. Engaging with people on their terms is something that journalism in general needs more of.”

“The importance of this type of engagement is that it is culturally relevant to the communities that are being served by the digital radio station. That means that the relationship they build with folks in events like this one is not only set to last, but also fill in the gaps that residents have already spoken about when it comes to recreation, networking, and belonging,” she added. 

Pa’ La is not the only one taking to in-person efforts. 

  • THE CITY in New York City has been hosting “Open Newsroom” events since its founding to engage readers in public libraries across the city. Topics have included discussing everything from how to deal with climate change, accessing mental health resources, COVID education, to news literacy. 
  • The Charlotte Observer spent this year opening two “mobile newsrooms” in predominantly Black and Brown communities in the region. Lisa Vernon Sparks, the paper’s race, culture and community engagement editor, wrote for Better News: “Many people have never met a journalist, and many don’t understand how we work to produce the news, the choices we make. Not knowing who we are is the main fuel of distrust and we wanted to change that. We also wanted to hear what stories people thought were missing from the news cycle and to do a better job of covering these communities more completely, beyond parachuting in when something bad happens.” 
  • The AFRO, the longest-running African American, family-owned newspaper business in the country, has been leveraging the Subtext tool to employ two-way texting its readers to get a better grasp of what they want to see covered and what are their current concerns. 
  • The San Diego Union-Tribune is hosting a whole Festival of Journalism to engage and grow its audience. The day-long event on October 14 features panels and other events so community members can meet the staff reporting about them. 

News from Inside the Community Media Sector

By now most of the media world has heard about the Press Forward coalition, led by the MacArthur and Knight Foundations, committing $500 million to invest in and save local news. However, Tracie Powell, founder of The Pivot Fund, and Dr. Meredith Clark, director of Northeastern University’s Center for Communication, Media Innovation and Social Change, point out that this is not the panacea the journalism world may think it is. Co-signed by several journalism organizations who represent journalists of color, the pair wrote an open letter stating that while the announcement from Press Forward is something to champion: “Philanthropy has a responsibility to be inclusive, intentional and transparent about how funding from this initiative is distributed. It is no secret that BIPOC-led organizations are awarded less grant money and are less trusted with how to spend that money when compared to white-led institutions.”

Fellowships & Opportunities

The Pulitzer Center provides grants to cover the hard costs of reporting projects. The only broad parameter is that projects address global systemic crises. The Pulitzer Center supports projects, not just one-off stories. The overarching mission is to raise awareness of underreported global crises, so the most successful applications demonstrate that the journalist has thought about how to reach the widest possible audience. Deadline is rolling. 

Applications for IRE's Chauncey Bailey Journalist of Color Investigative Reporting Fellowship are now being accepted. The year-long program is intended to prepare and support a journalist of color for a solid career in investigative reporting. The program also provides an opportunity for the fellow’s news outlets to benefit from the fellow’s investigative skill-building. Deadline is October 5. 

Applications are now open for the Fall 2023 McGraw Fellowships for Business Journalism from Newmark J-School’s McGraw Center for Business Journalism. The grants, up to $15,000, support projects that will produce a significant investigative or enterprise story providing fresh insight into an important business, financial or economic topic. Deadline October 6.

Stanford University’s Bill Lane Center for the American West is offering one Western Journalism and Media Fellowship to support a journalistic project that examines a crucial aspect of the West, its land, its people, its history, and the impact of the forces that power its economies. Deadline October 9.

Neal Peirce Foundation Journalism Travel Grants support journalists in covering under-told stories about ways to make cities and their metro regions work better for all their people. The foundation anticipates awarding up to seven grants of up to $1,500 in 2023. Deadline October 16.

The Jewish Writers’ Initiative is accepting applications for its Digital Storytellers Lab, a yearlong fellowship that gives digital-first storytellers the opportunity to develop, launch and grow original audio/visual media exploring Jewish themes for new media platforms. Deadline October 30.

The Open Notebook is accepting applications for its 2024 Early-Career Fellowship Program, which is open to science journalists with fewer than three years of regular professional science writing experience. Fellows receive a $6,000 stipend. Deadline October 31.

The American Chemical Society is accepting nominations for the James T. Grady-James H. Stack Award for Interpreting Chemistry for the Public. The award recognizes outstanding reporting directly to the public which materially increases the public's knowledge and understanding of chemistry, chemical engineering, and related fields. Deadline November

Biographers International Organization is accepting applications for the Frances “Frank” Rollin Fellowship awards $5,000 each to two authors working on a biographical work about an African American figure or figures whose story provides a significant contribution to our understanding of the Black experience. Deadline is February 1, 2024.
