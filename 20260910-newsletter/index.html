In community and ethnic media, local reporting does far more than deliver the news; it anchors civic life and preserves public trust. Pew Research finds that 85% of Americans believe local news outlets are vital to community well-being, and 71% say their local journalists report accurately. Yet translating that trust into long-term institutional resilience remains the central challenge facing community media. As newsrooms navigate everything from high-stakes coverage cycles to shifting digital platforms, an essential question remains at the center of the work: How do we build permanent, trusted connections with our audiences rather than relying on short-term outreach or reactive translation? 

That focus carries through this edition. In our Good Reads, we highlight three essential perspectives on community agency and audience infrastructure: Daniela Tabata (Audience Almanac) analyzes why bilingual newsrooms must treat non-English languages as core products. Carla Murphy (About the American Mainstream) challenges the media ecosystem to move past flat, one-dimensional depictions of immigrant communities. Lyric Aquino (Underscore Native News) uncovers how pop-culture fandom is directing real-world funding toward the Quileute Tribe’s climate relocation in Washington state.

Good Reads

The Playbook: What Newsrooms Can Learn From Telemundo and Mamdani About Engaging Bilingual Audiences | Daniela Tabata (Audience Almanac)

  • The Blueprint: Writing for Audience Almanac, Daniela Tabata analyzes the contrasting strategies behind Telemundo’s record-breaking World Cup viewership (where one-fifth of the audience did not speak Spanish) and the New York City Mayor's Office launching direct Spanish-language WhatsApp and Instagram channels. The piece breaks down four practical operational shifts for publishers: treating non-English languages as core products rather than after-the-fact translation layers; building parallel bilingual distribution routes; localizing language by specific cultural community rather than treating "Spanish" as a monolith; and partnering within existing community information ecosystems.
  • The Gain: Ethnic and community publishers serving immigrant, multilingual, or dual-language audiences gain an immediate self-audit framework. Rather than exhausting scarce newsroom resources on sporadic translation bursts, this framework helps editorial leaders design repeatable workflows, empower language-fluent editors to shape strategy, and distribute reporting through trusted, native channels where audiences already gather.
  • Read the full Audience Almanac analysis here.

Immigrants, Quiet Rebellion and “the Media” | Carla Murphy (About the American Mainstream)

  • The Blueprint: Veteran journalist and media educator Carla Murphy examines how modern journalism, media academia, and philanthropy hollow out the concept of "community." Drawing on her Caribbean-American diaspora background and decades inside newsrooms, Murphy unpacks the quiet rebellion of an estimated 80–90 million new Americans and second-generation immigrants who tune out mainstream media that flattens them into one-dimensional stereotypes or superficial marketing tropes. She argues that journalism’s democratic future hinges on moving past the "single story" to reflect the friction-filled, pluralist realities of how diaspora communities actually live.
  • The Gain: Essential reading for community and ethnic media founders and publishers. Murphy offers an incisive critique of performative newsroom diversity, urging media leaders and funders to stop treating immigrant communities as monolithic audiences and start building civic journalism that honors their internal complexity, nuance, and agency.
  • Read the full essay on About the American Mainstream.

Twilight Fandom Helps Fund Quileute Tribe’s Move to Higher Ground | Lyric Aquino (Underscore Native News)

  • The Blueprint: On the Washington coast, the Quileute Tribe is actively executing its multi-phase "Move to Higher Ground" project to relocate tribal schools, community infrastructure, and homes out of severe coastal erosion and active tsunami hazard zones. Despite the multi-billion-dollar Twilight franchise co-opting the Tribe’s name, likeness, and sacred origin stories without compensation, the Tribe received none of the commercial windfall. In response, dedicated fan networks are organizing grassroots giving campaigns to redirect financial resources directly into the Tribe’s sovereign climate relocation and community resilience funds.
  • The Gain: An exceptional case study in narrative accountability and Indigenous sovereignty. It illustrates how community-rooted newsrooms can investigate cultural extraction and climate resilience simultaneously, proving that local journalism can bridge national pop culture phenomena with local civic solutions.
  • Read the full story at Underscore Native News.

Fault Lines Reporting Fund | International Women’s Media Foundation (IWMF)

  • The Blueprint: A national reporting grant program offering awards ranging from $2,500 to $10,000 (with larger amounts considered case-by-case) to support staff and freelance journalists pursuing investigative, evidence-based reporting within the United States. The fund specifically resources coverage examining the forces driving racism, antisemitism, Islamophobia, misogyny, and other forms of intolerance, tracking how false, divisive, and harmful narratives take hold and impact marginalized communities. Funds cover travel, public records access, field logistics, security, insurance, and reporting-related expenses. Open to journalists with 3+ years of professional experience; applications must be in English, but reporting may be published in any language in a U.S. outlet within one year.
  • The Gain: Ethnic, immigrant, and community newsrooms frequently bear the brunt of hate-fueled disinformation campaigns targeting their neighborhoods. This fund equips independent reporters and local outlets with the critical financial and safety runway needed to produce deep, rigorous accountability reporting on extremist networks and systemic intolerance without draining general operating budgets.
