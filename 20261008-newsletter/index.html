The 2022 merger of Chicago’s WBEZ and the Chicago Sun-Times may seem unrelated to the challenges facing community and ethnic media. However, in her interview on Medill’s State of Local News podcast, Chicago Public Media CEO Melissa Bell outlines operational problems that closely mirror the financial and structural difficulties independent, immigrant, and diaspora publishers face day to day.

Four years after merging Chicago’s NPR affiliate with the daily newspaper to create the largest nonprofit newsroom in the country, Bell is now leading the organization as its first five-year period of $61 million in philanthropic funding comes to an end. Bell’s challenges include renegotiating contracts with print vendors, carrying out staff buyouts that resulted in about 35 jobs being cut, navigating a reduction in federal funding, and shifting from that initial philanthropic capital to a sustainable revenue model from memberships, sponsorships, and print subscriptions.

Community and ethnic newsrooms regularly face the same problem with foundation grants: they usually last only two or three years and are not always renewed, forcing publishers to find new ways to keep their operations going. Like Bell, many diaspora publications need to support both print or ethnic radio for long-time audiences and digital content for younger readers. When funding shifts or costs go up, the main question is how to keep essential community reporting strong without stretching teams too thin or losing editorial independence.

In the Good Reads section, Sarah Alvarez (News Fix) explains why newsrooms serving underrepresented groups should go beyond detached reporting and create practical information tools to promote mutual aid and civic engagement. Kyndell Harkness from the American Press Institute suggests three simple methods for reaching people who avoid the news: listening projects, SMS and zines, and beat swaps to reduce burnout. To help publishers navigate changing funding sources, Poynter’s course, Ethically Funding Local News, outlines how foundations invest in the media and includes templates for editorial firewalls and negotiating operational support. The Local Media Association’s updated NewsFuel directory now contains listings of 205 local media funders.

Good Reads

News Fix #63: The Lack of Solidarity in News Is a Case for Civic Media | Sarah Alvarez (News Fix)

  • The Blueprint: In her weekly newsletter News Fix, Outlier Media founder Sarah Alvarez looks at how traditional newsrooms often focus too much on conflict and rarely offer reporting that helps people work together. She compares these common news practices to the wider world of civic media, which includes libraries, public access channels, and hybrid newsrooms like The Jersey Bee, Capital B, and Planet Detroit. Using Communication Infrastructure Theory, Alvarez argues that local information sources should give people practical tools to support each other, work together effectively, and take part in civic life.
  • The Gain: For community and ethnic media publishers, the essay offers a way to think about newsroom purpose that goes beyond just tracking audience numbers or pointing out problems. Alvarez encourages editors to see local news as a part of civic infrastructure, focusing on whether their reporting helps residents work together and solve issues as a community.
  • Read the full News Fix essay here.

3 Experiments to Improve Engagement With News Avoiders | Kyndell Harkness (American Press Institute)

  • The Blueprint: Published by the American Press Institute under its Revenue & Resilience initiative, consultant and former Minnesota Star Tribune editor Kyndell Harkness outlines three newsroom exercises to reach audiences that tune out conventional reporting. Harkness explains how teams can match listening projects to organizational capacity, test alternative delivery vehicles such as zines, SMS text messages, and audio on recurring story formats to find what information feels like community care. They can also implement internal beat swaps to break reporting silos and reduce staff burnout on high-intensity beats.
  • The Gain: Editors and publishers in smaller newsrooms can use this guide to run practical workshops that test audience engagement without adding extra work for their teams. It explains how to try new ways to share stories, gather community feedback, and help reporters learn about related topics.
  • Read the full American Press Institute guide here.

Chicago Public Media CEO Melissa Bell is Leading the Nation’s Most Watched Local News Experiment | Tim Franklin (Local News Initiative, Northwestern University)

  • The Blueprint: In an interview on Medill’s State of Local News podcast, host Tim Franklin speaks with Chicago Public Media CEO Melissa Bell on the progress and financial realities four years into the landmark 2022 merger of NPR affiliate WBEZ and the Chicago Sun-Times. Bell details the concrete steps taken to stabilize the nation's largest nonprofit newsroom as its initial five-year, $61 million philanthropic backing winds down—including renegotiating print vendor contracts, navigating workforce buyouts that cut approximately 35 positions, and responding to federal funding reductions. She explains the organization’s multi-revenue strategy spanning memberships, sponsorships, and print subscriptions, alongside digital experiments like Chicago.com.
  • The Gain: Bell gives publishers and media executives a clear look at what happens after a merger when considering nonprofit conversions, mergers, or philanthropic funding. The discussion covers the challenges of blending broadcast and print cultures, handling staff changes, and moving from large initial donations to steady earned income.
  • Read the transcript and listen to the podcast at the Local News Initiative here.

Ethically Funding Local News: A Practical Course | The Poynter Institute

  • The Blueprint: A free online course you can take at your own pace, created by Poynter faculty member Megan Griffith-Greene with help from the Knight Foundation and Press Forward. The course has 9 lessons, 47 topics, and takes about 90 minutes to complete. It helps philanthropic funders learn how to support local journalism while preserving editorial independence. The curriculum includes short video lessons with leaders from organizations like the Barancik Foundation and Appalachia Funders Network, interactive reflection exercises, and case studies from newsrooms such as Outlier Media, Documenters, The Big Bend Sentinel, and The Baltimore Banner. Key topics include setting up editorial firewalls, providing operational support beyond funding stories, creating funder guidelines, and measuring newsroom impact.
  • The Gain: Community and ethnic newsroom publishers can learn firsthand how institutional and regional philanthropists are taught to fund media. By looking at the course curriculum and funder templates, newsroom leaders get trusted language to set up editorial firewalls, ask for operational funding beyond content, and set clear ethical boundaries when talking with donors.
  • Enroll in Ethically Funding Local News for free at Poynter here.
