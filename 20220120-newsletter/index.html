Community Broadcasters Lead the Charge for Inclusivity

As the Great Resignation of 2021 continues to wash over the media landscape, a wider story of racial injustice has emerged, most recently in the very public reckoning taking place in public media. CCM sat down with Sally Kane, CEO of the National Federation of Community Broadcasters (NFCB), to understand some of the shifting context around diversity, inclusivity, community radio, and public media. NFCB serves 202 community radio stations across the country.

From your vantage point at the helm of NFCB, and as a broadcaster of more than 25 years yourself, how do you understand this current reckoning underway in public media?
Public media’s business model has unfortunately encouraged over-serving a white, overeducated elite, and it is difficult to get off that train because it butters our bread. I know these work cultures are fraying around the pressures of overserving this audience, but it seems to me that a lot of these conversations, this very powerful vulnerability, is only possible because public media is willing to take a hard look at itself. Who was it that said, “Human beings yearn for more than a chicken in every pot.”

And that’s why we’re here. Increasingly, “the media” has become a weapon and there’s also been a shift in the language to call everything media. And you have to dig deep to know what journalism is. In public media, we have chosen as an industry to do this digging, and to go into these difficult conversations.

The deep fractures in our society really call for some form of structured reconciliation nationwide in my view, and public radio – but specifically community radio has a really powerful role to play in that.


There is a rich tradition of broadcasting in Black media – how is Black-led community radio figuring into this reckoning?

Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) have been a powerhouse zone for Black radio, and HBCUs are experiencing a renaissance right now. Many HBCUs were hanging on by a thread financially for years and there’s a beautiful revitalization of these institutions going on, in part due to the larger context of reckoning for racial justice. Institutionally, HBCU administrations are often really behind their radio stations. Through this revitalization, what I hope we’ll see is younger people coming in and getting involved in broadcasting at HBCUs, and then starting to move on to more leadership positions in public media. There is a lot of turnover in these industries, so that is an opportunity.


You’ve said that a lot of broadcasters of color are finding their way into podcasting, given the limited number of host positions in public radio, but radio remains one of the few places where immigrant communities can find local news in their own languages. How can radio continue to serve immigrant communities?
Historically, community stations have been a patchwork quilt of fairly diverse programming. They were Facebook before Facebook. More recently, LPFM stations have filled an urgent need in immigrant communities in a very effective way. For example, the Somali community in the Twin Cities has really mobilized around low-power FM radio, where a central concern is getting safety information out to people arriving from warmer climates so they don’t freeze in the winter and other basic information.
 
Radio is still the most economical thing going for serving basic information needs on a local level.

Some locally focused stations have difficulty finding space for this important programming, because they are bound by terms required by nationally syndicated content producers to air a certain number of hours of the programming they subscribe to. The more local the better at this point, I feel. 


The Digital Equity Act passed last November on the heels of several years of organizing. How have Tribal radio leaders really led the charge for broadband access and digital equity?

Broadband access happens through the National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) [an executive agency]. While the NTIA’s Public Telecommunications Facilities Program – a competitive grant program that allocated up to $40 million to broadcasters – went away under the Obama administration, broadband access work didn’t. Together with the Office of Native Affairs and Policy at the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), Tribal radio really pushed on the broadband front. Although broadband access has improved generally on Tribal lands, Tribal radio licensees have lost a lot of resources to support basic infrastructure.


NFCB is really leading the charge on a lot of the work to make public media a more inclusive space, not just in your public support for Public Media For All, but through your efforts to help your member stations build out governance strategies rooted in diversity, equity, and inclusion. How are you approaching this work with your members?

Community radio stations were anchored in the counterculture movement of the late 1960s, so if you ask “How are you showing up as an ally today,” many folks will think, our role is to be a station that amplifies counterculture voices and that is how we create diversity. This is no longer a solid argument and can be a barrier to evolving the notion of what diversity and public service means in the present context. 

Early public radio was truly radical, it was about taking a field recorder and harvesting what was being spoken and amplifying it. But over the years, public radio as an industry has become quietly successful without people noticing it, and the problem with super-serving a certain audience is that now you’ve become a kind of establishment yourself. Our role at NFCB is to question that, and to advocate for and encourage public radio content that reflects our core mission of public service and attention to civic life, public safety, and education. Our responsibility is to keep the “public” in public media.
Black Media Product Strategy: Open for Applications

On January 17, as the country celebrated the life and legacy of Dr. Martin Luther King, we launched Black Media Product Strategy, a new program to support and sustain Black media – the keepers of Black history across the U.S. Developed in collaboration with our colleagues at J+, Newmark J-School’s center for executive, professional, and entrepreneurial journalism programs, this new initiative invites teams of three from up to 10 Black-owned media outlets to develop revenue growth and audience engagement strategies built around news products, like newsletters, podcasts, special issues, and more. The six-month-long online program is tuition free, with generous support from the John S. and James L. Knight Foundation. Read more and apply here.
