Channeling AI to empower communities and the journalists who serve them

Our 2025 AI Community Engagement cohort at the Newmark J-School with J+ Senior Program Manager Kyle Plantz (R)

Community journalists are becoming AI pioneers with the help of an inaugural cohort for the AI Community Engagement Lab, a partnership between CCM and Newmark J-School’s J+. In March, eighteen media leaders from diverse communities gathered in New York to begin a seven-month cohort education experience exploring how artificial intelligence can transform local newsrooms with limited resources. The program aims to put powerful new tools in the hands of those who need them most.

AI and new technology can be intimidating to those who do not regularly work in a technical field, but Marie Gilot, Executive Director of J+, said that was not a concern for this “fearless” group of journalists dedicated to serving their communities. 

“It is my experience that community journalists and local journalists are much more nimble when it comes to experimenting with generative AI than journalists from larger, more resourced newsrooms that may only permit staff in a special team to use AI. Ironically, this means that community journalists are ahead of the curve when it comes to understanding generative AI,” she said. 

CCM’s Communications Director Jehangir Khattak agreed and said that as AI advances, community media newsrooms are learning about it and “find efficient and creative ways to deploy it in newsrooms.” For Khattak, this program “will fill the gaps that exist in newsrooms concerning the use of AI” to help the leaders with engagement and making their businesses more sustainable. 

The goals are similar throughout the cohort, which  represents a diverse set of communities across the U.S. and come from backgrounds in Ecuador, Ethiopia, Haiti, India, Lithuania, Mexico, Nepal, Nigeria, and Venezuela: Identifying  if AI tools can help save their news operations money and understanding how these tools can help them better engage with members of their communities and audience. Through virtual classes, hands-on workshops, online coaching, and a final project, participants hope to walk away with a better understanding of both. 

Khasokhas and NepYork editor Suresh Shahi believes learning how to leverage AI through the training program “will empower our [Nepali] community.” He said he hopes to be able to use AI for “streamlining content creation, improving accessibility, and personalizing reader experiences.” He also appreciated “joining a forward-thinking community committed to innovation and growth.” 

It is that sense of having a journalism community that fueled other cohort members as well. Christian De Jesus Betancourt, the Bilingual Communities Issues Reporter with Central Valley Journalism Collaborative/The Merced FOCUS, said “being part of this cohort has been energizing — it’s rare to find a space where journalists from so many communities can build, question, and imagine together.” Betancourt also hopes to harness the power of AI to connect with the bilingual audience of The Merced “by making information more accessible and responsive to their needs.” 

Running small community media newsrooms’ editorial and business operations can be isolating for journalists professionally, despite the deep connections to their community. Claudia Yaujar-Amaro, Editor in Chief of Planeta Venus Online was also excited to find a space of collaboration within the cohort. "Meeting new people who care for similar things I care about, who are working hard to empower their communities with reliable information, motivates me to continue the work,” she said.

“I've been and still am somewhat of an AI-skeptic,” Valerie Osier, Managing Editor of RANGE Media in Spokane, Washington, said. However, she felt the cohort “has already helped me gain a better understanding of AI as a tool for change — especially for small, under-resourced newsrooms. There's so much work that newsrooms need to do behind the scenes to keep the lights on that distracts from the directly impactful work of our journalism, so I'm optimistic we can use it as a tool to save brain power for that big work.”

Still, using AI can be daunting for many journalists and Gilot offered some advice to get started to explore how it can help community-based newsrooms. “You don't have anything to lose if you keep in mind the three main principles of ethical AI use: 1) human in the loop (check the work of robots before you publish), 2) don't put sensitive materials in [a large language model], and 3) transparency (tell your audience when and how you use generative AI).” 

She also warned that community media leaders may “have a lot to lose by not getting to know the technology now that it's still open and free.” 

The Lab will run from March 17 to October 21, 2025, with a summer break and conclude with final project presentations. This program would not be possible without the support of the Walton Family Foundation.
