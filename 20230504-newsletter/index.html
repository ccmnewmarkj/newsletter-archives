The future of community media

CCM collaborated with National Trust for Local News (NTLN) on research about the sustainability of community media outlets, funded largely by The Lenfest Institute for Journalism.

The report compiled results of a survey conducted of 103 leaders of community media outlets serving racial, ethnic, or immigrant communities in 24 states. These leaders were asked about their concerns regarding the future of their publications and the types of resources, funding, and change they would like to see and need in order to make their publications more sustainable. 

One of the main takeaways from this report is that 53% of community media outlets surveyed said they expected to go out of business within five years if current revenue trends continue. As CCM Communications Director Jehangir Khattak pointed out this is a “shocking reminder about the level of vulnerability this media faces in a largely weak ecosystem.” 

Much of what we learned from the survey responses was regarding financial issues. Among them was that most outlets had trouble with putting a plan in place for the future once independent owners and publishers want to get out of the media business. Khattak explained: "The report NTLN produced on the basis of this groundbreaking research is an important document which sheds light on the most often overlooked issue of succession planning in the community media landscape.”

While we also saw that many publishers' main source of funding comes from grants and individual gifts, a majority of publishers recognized the need for access to technology and digital transformation tools as a key factor in ensuring their sustainability. 

There is, however, hope for the future of community media, as the survey responses showed. The publishers who responded saying they were confident in their outlet’s ability to last five years or longer had a few things in common that set them apart: allocating more staff to sales and marketing efforts and having leadership who had more of a background in running a business versus running a newsroom.

A powerful inter-community event on a shared problem: Gender-based violence

This week we were proud to host the first collaboration between our Black and Asian Media Initiatives, led by Cheryl Thompson-Morton and Kavitha Rajagopalan, respectively. You can watch a recording of the webinar. 

The event was a powerful discussion of an engrained problem in both communities: gender-based violence and the role of community media in covering it. Panelists included Rebekah Duvall-Taylor, Executive Director, Black Women Lead: Black Femme Fund; Jeehae Fischer, Executive Director, Korean American Family Service Center; Yasmeen Hamza, CEO, Womankind; Zainab Muzaffar, Senior Associate Director of Communications and Development, Sakhi for South Asian Women; and Shenikqwa Phillip, Program Manager, Black Women in Motion.

These experts from both communities shared their work with attendees but also their best practices for protecting survivors of gender-based violence in environments where silence and shame are not just common but encouraged and perpetuated. As Rajagopalan said, not only was the event “a powerful reflection of how much more Black and Asian communities have in common than what differs between us [but also]  just how much can be gained by activating our in-language and community media not just to connect survivors with life-saving resources, but in dismantling our shared cultures of silence, victim-blaming and shame.” Hamza also noted that especially when it comes to in-language media, coverage of these stories leads to an uptick in callers to her organization’s helpline, reduces isolation and shame, and decreases stigma around seeking help. 

One of the most informative aspects of the webinar were the tips on how best community media journalists can cover these types of stories, which are often under-reported or done so without considering the survivors' mental health or safety. 

As Phillip explained “media literacy is so important” when reporting on gender-based violence. She noted media can often perpetuate rape culture and normalize GBV that is victim-blaming, shaming, and set in a male gaze.

Some of the tips shared include: 

  • Avoiding cultural stereotypes
  • Not assuming anything including gender, religion, race, ethnicity, or socio-economic status
  • "We also ask that reporters don't ask our survivors to repeat their story over and over again...respecting boundaries and taking time,” from Sakhi’s Muzaffar 
  • Phillip also asked journalists to consider their own roles in telling the story. In the interest of avoiding performative allyship -- you may not be the right outlet or reporter to cover a particular story. Advocates can help you determine the best way forward.

Thompson-Morton is “excited to have more opportunities to have intercommunity conversations…learning about solutions coming from other communities can help us improve outcomes for society at large.” 

In the fall, ahead of Domestic Violence Awareness Month, Rajagopalan and Thompson-Morton will publish a resource guide with tipsheets, reporting tools, recent research and data, expert interview sources, and joint training opportunities for Asian American and Black community media journalists. 

You can read more about the collaboration here and watch the full recording here. 

News from Inside the Community Media Sector

According to a recent Gallup poll, Americans' trust in media remains near a record low. Just 7% of Americans have "a great deal" of trust and confidence in the media, and 27% have "a fair amount." (We have included some more information on this down below in our Tools & Tips section as well.)

Politico's founder is spending $20 million to train aspiring journalists through his newly-established Allbritton Journalism Institute (AJI), a non-profit educational organization and newsroom that will train reporters to produce fact-based, non-partisan journalism on government and politics.

OP-ED: The publisher of the Times of San Diego writes about a proposed bill in California that would force Google, Microsoft and Facebook to pay usage fees for the news articles they index. He noted the bill is intended to preserve journalism, but the unintended consequence could be to stifle innovation in local media.

Here’s an essay on what one journalism organization employee learned about audience retention strategies while unsubscribing their group from 22 digital newspaper subscriptions. 

With the dismantling of so much of Twitter’s corporate infrastructure and platform features - and subsequent usefulness - there are a slew of disaster alert accounts preparing for a world after Twitter.

Birmingham Watch, part of Alabama Initiative for Independent Journalism, asks the question “If you’re a reporter, how sneaky are you willing to be?” in a piece outlining some real-life scenarios of reporters having to pause and think about actions that while not illegal, may be in a gray area - and definitely produce important stories. 

Newmark J-School’s J+, in collaboration with News Product Alliance and News Catalyst, announced this month a new product training program for journalists, the News Product Management Certification (NPMC). Mid to senior-level news professionals who want to take on more product responsibilities in their organizations should sign up for the mailing list to receive news about the program. This will be a rigorous, part-time, and virtual program that will bring together a diverse cohort of 25-30 participants from across the global news industry. Applications will open later in the Fall and information sessions will be held in June. Support for the program is being provided in part by the Knight-Lenfest Local News Transformation Fund. 

Indiana News Now! launched earlier this month and provides radio stations and online media in the state free access to high quality, public interest news. The new program from Public News Service, a national network of news producers, is part of a larger effort in Indiana to fill gaps in news coverage of rural areas. 

There is a growing concern about the effects of artificial intelligence on writers and the journalism industry as a whole. Bloomberg reports on how AI chatbots have been used to create dozens of news content farms. For more background information, here is a primer on copyright law and generative AI for news media.

American Press Institute is helping news organizations reimagine local opinion journalism to promote healthier civic discourse and to better understand its role in news business sustainability. This essay is part of a series of essays on the topic, and explores how opinion editors might create space for deeper engagement that helps residents overcome local division.

Fellowships & Opportunities

The Local Media Association is accepting applications for its new sustainability lab for family-owned, independent local media, with support from the Google News Initiative. Directors of the LMA Family and Independent Media Sustainability Lab — FIMS Lab for short — and a team of expert consultants will work for one year with key leaders of 12 family-owned and independent local media companies, immersing in strategic business transformation work intended to advance them toward long-term sustainability and financial independence. Deadline is May 5.  

Colorado Public Radio is accepting applications for the Max Wycisk News Fellow for Digital Audiences. Fellows will learn about attracting, earning trust of, and developing relationships with audiences They will produce clean and confidence-inspiring digital content on multiple platforms. The fellowship is specifically designed to support recent graduates and early career journalists, and folks looking to pivot into this field. Deadline is May 5. 

Altavoz Lab is accepting applications for its 2023 Environmental Fellowship, designed to produce collaborative projects to strengthen bilingual (English and Spanish) reporters at community outlets that serve Black, Indigenous, immigrant and other communities of color in the U.S. with the goal of publishing stories that will enable local audiences to participate fully in democracy. Deadline is May 15.

MuckRock is looking for a FOIA Fellow to make transparency their beat. The selected candidate will cover key developments in public records, helping respond to thorny question challenges while assisting our newsrooms around the country with our collaborative reporting efforts. Deadline is May 19. 

Applications open for Isaac Rauch Fellowship at Documented. The NYC-based outlet is seeking an ambitious reporter for a six-month fellowship that seeks to uncover stories that matter to immigrant New Yorkers. Deadline is May 19. 

The 19th is recruiting for its 2023-2024 fellowship cohort of the Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Fellowship. Launched in partnership with Nikole Hannah-Jones, Howard University’s Knight chair in race and journalism, it will provide recent graduates, mid-career alums, and former students of Historically Black Colleges and Universities with full-year salaried and benefit-laden fellowships in the areas of reporting, editing, audience engagement and news product and technology. Deadline is May 31.

Grist is accepting submissions for Imagine 2200: Write the future, its climate fiction short story contest. This is an invitation to writers from all over the globe to imagine a future in which solutions to the climate crisis flourish and help bring about radical improvements to our world. Deadline is June 13.
