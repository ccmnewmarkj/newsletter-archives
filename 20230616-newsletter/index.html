Covering hate crimes in your community 
 From L to R: BMI Director Cheryl Thompson-Morton; Jason Villemez of Philadelphia Gay News; Christina Carrega of Capital B; Yiyan Zheng of World Journal; and Philissa Cramer of the Jewish Telegraph Agency
Last week, CCM hosted a symposium on covering hate crimes in Black, Asian, LGBTQ, and Jewish communities. You can watch the recording here: Part 1 and Part 2.

We were honored to hear such a nuanced, impactful conversation, especially since Newmark J-School Dean Graciela Mochkofsky, who previously led CCM, kicked off the symposium. We were fortunate enough to learn from experts like Maya Wiley, president and CEO of The Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights and The Leadership Conference Education Fund, and Tamara K. Nopper, a sociologist and senior researcher with the Labor Futures initiative at Data & Society. 

Wiley spoke about how stories of hate crimes are often only covered by community media journalists until a larger media outlet picks it up, emphasizing the importance of these stories and outlets. Nopper also discussed the need for better data literacy and narratives among community media journalists in order to better serve their audiences. 

The symposium also included a panel discussion with Black, Asian, Jewish, and LGBTQ community media journalists covering hate crimes in their communities: Philissa Cramer of the Jewish Telegraphic Agency; Jason Villemez, Editor of the Philadelphia Gay News; Yiyan Zheng, a reporter at World Journal, the largest Chinese-language daily newspaper in the U.S.; and Christina Carrega, the National Criminal Justice Reporter for Capital B. 

Villemez discussed the need to not rely solely on law enforcement narratives when reporting on hate crimes. Carrega explained there is no requirement, only a recommendation, that law enforcement flag a hate crime to the public. However, there are requirements for journalists covering these stories within their own communities like understanding which community voices to cite and the cultural, ethnic, and historical factors at hand. Cramer went through a list of questions that need to be asked when covering an unfolding story like a hate crimes incident: What are the facts right now? What is our community saying? What kind of context can we provide? Zheng spoke about the crucial need for community media journalists to cover stories of identity, specifically citing coverage of AAPI-targeted crimes during the pandemic and the flattening of identity in mainstream news outlets. 

As Kavitha Rajagopalan, Director of the Asian Media Initiative, put it, “this was an important step toward bringing solidarity and mutual understanding of how hate crimes function in different contexts to community journalism.” 

The third component of our symposium consisted of workshops intended to assist attendees with putting knowledge into practice and led by Heather E. Murray, Managing Attorney of the Local Journalism Project at the Cornell Law School First Amendment Clinic; and Michael Kilian, the New York state editor for Gannett and executive editor of the Rochester Democrat and Chronicle. 

“Despite the distinctions that often can divide the communities we serve there is a lot that we have in common,” Black Media Initiative Director Cheryl Thompson-Morton pointed out. 

“This event illustrated how a culture of hate impacts various marginalized communities, and also shows how we can improve our situation through solidarity,” she noted. You can watch the recording in two parts here and here.

The Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism at the University of Oxford has published its Digital News Report 2023. The report assessed digital media consumption in 46 markets - representing nearly half of the world - based on a YouGov survey of over 93,000 online news consumers. “The report documents how video-based content, distributed via networks such as TikTok, Instagram and YouTube are becoming more important for news, especially in parts of the Global South, while legacy platforms such as Facebook are losing influence.” In a related piece, the Press Gazette reports on the data from publisher analytics firm Chartbeat which shows Twitter’s shrinking role as traffic source for news publishers.

Fellowships & Opportunities

The Fund for Equity in Local News, a project of the Alternative Newsweekly Foundation, is hiring several coaches for the first phase of a new training program designed to help increase the digital revenue capabilities of publishers from National Newspaper Publishers Association (NNPA), the National Association of Hispanic Publications (NAHP) and the Association of Alternative Newsmedia (AAN). Coaches will meet regularly with 8-10 publishers to help them launch a revenue or growth experiment within the 90-day training program. Deadline is June 15 at 11 pm PST.

INDY Week is seeking two new reporting fellows to cover culture and politics in the Research Triangle, NC area. The fellowship positions are supported by two INDY partners: Scalawag, a Black-led and -centering Southern journalism and storytelling organization, and The Assembly, a digital magazine focused on deep reporting about power and place in North Carolina. Rolling deadline begins June 15. 

NPR’s Next Gen Radio is a five-day, audio-focused digital journalism project. The hybrid program is designed to give competitively selected participants the opportunity to learn how to report and produce a non-narrated audio piece and a companion multimedia story. Those chosen for the program are paired with a professional journalist, who serves as their mentor for the week, and the chosen participant is paid a stipend for their work.

The Society of Professional Journalists is accepting applications for its Eugene C. Pulliam Fellowship for Editorial Writing. Established to enable a mid-career editorial writer or columnist to have time away from daily responsibilities for study and research, the cash award allows Pulliam Editorial Fellows to take courses, pursue independent study, travel, and pursue other endeavors that enrich their knowledge of a public interest issue. Deadline is June 19. 

Jewish Currents, a magazine of the Jewish left, is seeking a fellow for a remote one-year position, to begin in September. You do not have to be Jewish – or a current expert in Jewish politics – to be right for this job, though you must demonstrate a familiarity with their work and position in the media landscape. Applicants from marginalized identities, including identities marginalized in the Jewish community (Jews of color, Sephardi and Mizrahi Jews, queer and trans people), are encouraged to apply. Rolling deadline is June 23. 

The Pulitzer Center, in collaboration with Diversify Photo, is taking applications for its Eyewitness Photojournalism Grant. This grant aims to tell visual stories about and by photojournalists from historically underrepresented communities. Deadline is June 30.

The International Women’s Media Foundation is accepting its next round of applications for the Howard G. Buffett Fund for Women Journalists, which supports projects including educational opportunities, investigative reporting and media development initiatives. The IWMF is inclusive of all women, nonbinary and gender nonconforming journalists. The next round’s deadline is July 1. Applications are accepted on a monthly, rolling deadline and any submitted on or after July 1 will receive a decision in September.

The Bertha Foundation is taking applications for the Bertha Challenge, an opportunity for activists and investigative journalists to spend a year working on one pressing social justice challenge and to deliver a body of work at the end of the fellowship year. Successful applicants will receive non-residential paid fellowships and project budgets. Deadline is July 4. 

This year’s Photography Expanded Fellowship will support projects on the topic of heat and climate crisis that reimagine what a critical and ecological photographic practice might look like. Magnum Foundation is seeking proposals from photographers from all regions of the world who are producing work that responds to the theme and are excited to contribute to a creative community of fellows. Up to 12 selected participants will receive a grant of $10,000, take part in two workshops, and connect with collaborative partners and networks. Deadline is July 6.

Teen Vogue is taking applications from Student Correspondents to report on upcoming elections and voting rights in the following states: Nevada, Florida, Michigan, Wisconsin, Arizona, Georgia, and Pennsylvania. Apply by July 7. 

USC Annenberg’s Center for Health Journalism is accepting applications for its Data Fellowship. The program offers journalists an opportunity to transform their reporting by training them to “interview the data” as if it were a human source. Equipped with the tools to find original sources of information and perform data analysis, fellows graduate from this hands-on training program prepared to produce a major investigative or explanatory health reporting project in the months that follow. Deadline is July 17.

The Institute for Journalism & Natural Resources is taking applications for its Klamath River Institute. The program is an expenses-paid, six-day, seven-night Institute for up to 15 journalists that will explore the Klamath River Basin from its headwaters in southern Oregon to its mouth in northern California, and investigate how the removal of four dams will impact Indigenous rights and sovereignty, hydrology, food systems, agriculture practices, river ecosystems, fisheries, wildlife migrations, the energy system, and more. Deadline is July 28. 

The National Center on Disability and Journalism (NCDJ) is now accepting entries for the Katherine Schneider Journalism Award for Excellence in Reporting on Disability and the Gary Corcoran Student Prize for Excellence in Reporting on Disability. Entries are judged by professional journalists and experts on disability issues. Deadline is August 1. 

The Artist/Scholar in Residence program at the Library of Congress is now accepting applications. The program, part of LOC’s Connecting Communities Digital Initiative (CCDI) is part of the Library’s Of the People: Widening the Path program with support from the Mellon Foundation, supports artists or scholars in the creation of new scholarly and/or artists' works that imaginatively study, experiment with, and/or critique the Library’s digital collections, materials, and/or services. The program is open to a broad array of artists and scholars, including (but not limited to): visual artists, conceptual artists, dancers, journalists, cultural critics, independent scholars, academic scholars, and cultural heritage practitioners, including librarians. For 2023, the Library intends to award two 12-month grants of up to $90,000 each. Deadline is August 7.
