“I Pray and I Go”: Reporting ICE Raids in Louisiana’s Latino Neighborhoods

Welcome to the Center for Community Media’s Spotlight on Community Media newsletter! Once a month, you will see a Q+A, interview, case study, or other feature on a community media publisher or issue relevant to community-serving media outlets and those who support them. Our goal for this newsletter is to tell stories about the people who make up the community media ecosystem, how they work, their successes and lessons learned. Do you have a publisher or issue you would like to see featured? Email ccm@journalism.cuny.edu.

For more than two decades, Jambalaya News has been a critical news source for Louisiana’s Latino community. Based out of the New Orleans area, publisher Brenda Murphy now finds herself documenting the human cost of intensified Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) operations. 

The Center for Community Media spoke with Murphy about her effort to report directly from neighborhoods where raids unfold. She is guided not just by community tips but also by a deep sense of responsibility to protect vulnerable residents. In our conversation, Murphy describes the fear, grief, and resilience she witnesses daily, as well as the challenges of being a small outlet covering a crisis when government officials refuse to respond to requests for comment. 

CCM: For readers who may not know Jambalaya News, can you start by describing your audience and the kind of coverage you provide?

Murphy: Our primary audience is the Latino community—people from Honduras, Nicaragua, Guatemala, Mexico, El Salvador. We’ve been here 21 years, since July 2004, serving the Louisiana Latino community with local news. We try to be a bridge between the community and local government, covering political issues, education, and especially immigration. 

CCM: How are you approaching coverage of all the increased ICE activity right now?

Murphy: Most of our information comes directly from community members. They text, call, or send videos when a raid is happening in their neighborhood. When the “Catahoula Crunch” operation began last Wednesday, people immediately sent me an address where agents were trying to pull four Latino men off a roof. I drove there myself. ICE agents told me to move my car, but they never stopped me from recording. I stayed at a distance and did my job. I’m careful. I always wear my press badge and Jambalaya News shirt, and I keep in mind that I’m a permanent resident, not a U.S. citizen yet. I never know how ICE might react so I’m trying to respect them, but also do my job because I’m a journalist. 

CCM: Do you ever feel afraid while reporting?

Murphy: Yes. Every time I get a call and start driving toward a raid, I pray for protection. I pray, and I go. You feel fear because you see the injustice. Even people with permits or legal status being taken away, and ICE is saying: ‘We’ll figure it out later.’ But when neighbors see the media show up, I think it gives them a little strength. Still, the fear is real. 

CCM: We see all these stories about families being torn apart as a result of these raids.  Can you share what you’re seeing in Louisiana?

Murphy: I just met a grandmother caring for eight children. One son has already been deported, two others are in [immigration] jail, and the mother is in jail too, all because of ICE. The grandmother now has an eviction notice. It’s heartbreaking. I can write stories and bring food once or twice, but I can’t fix the larger crisis: people afraid to leave home, kids are missing school, there’s no money for rent or utilities. It’s overwhelming. 

CCM: Some outlets like El Tímpano in the Bay Area and Conecta Arizona, have formal processes to protect staff and sources, especially undocumented people. What are you doing in case those people also need help from the community, like that grandmother?

Murphy: We always ask permission. I record only their voice or from behind. Our main rule—especially now—is to protect the community and not put anyone at risk. If they give permission, we record; if not, we respect it. 

But with the grandmother, we explained the story to her and she gave full permission. She believes the only way to get help is to be open about her situation. Some families may even need long-term support or someone to “adopt” the family temporarily. Everything is with consent. 

CCM: Let’s talk about Governor Jeff Landry. How are you approaching the political side of this story?

Murphy: It’s been almost impossible. Republican officials in our area won’t respond to calls or give quotes. The only people consistently standing up for the community are Democrats like Congressman Troy Carter and a few local leaders. Since Governor Landry took office, undocumented residents, even those with Individual Taxpayer Identification Numbers who pay taxes to the state, can no longer apply for state assistance like rent or utility support like they had been able to before he came into office last year. But, we can’t get anyone on the Republican side to address it. So we report that they aren’t responding and that becomes the story. 

CCM: There are some readers of Jambalaya News that support perhaps the governor and the ICE raids. How do you cover that side? Are they from the community as well? 

Murphy: For now, it’s mostly comments on our posts; some are very hateful. We haven’t had anyone approach us directly to make their case, but if they did, I would interview them. As a journalist, I put my personal feelings aside just like I do every time I drive to a raid site. But so far, it’s only online reactions, not people willing to talk on the record. 

CCM: How are you managing this kind of coverage while trying to run the business side of Jambalaya News?

Murphy: It’s hard. The economy is bad, advertisers are few, and I mostly work alone. It’s usually just me, two phones, a tripod, a microphone, and God. After 21 years, we’ve survived Katrina and so much more. I tell myself: we will survive this too.
