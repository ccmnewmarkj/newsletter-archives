Black Narrative Power: Media 2070 Charts a Course for Reparative Journalism

L to R: Venneika Williams, Media 2070 Campaign Manager; Anshantia Oso, Media 2070 Senior Director

Visionaries Venneikia Williams and Tia Oso are transforming media landscapes as the driving forces behind Free Press' Media 2070 initiative. As Campaign Manager and Senior Director respectively, they dig into Black Narrative Power Month, media reparations, and the importance of Black community media. In our Black History Month conversation, Williams and Oso illuminated the critical importance of Black community media while highlighting their latest resource—a comprehensive wellness toolkit for Black media makers featuring curated playlists, illuminating essays, and practical resources supporting both personal wellbeing and the revolutionary practice of reparative journalism.

CCM: What is Media 2070? Why do you call it that?

Venneikia Williams: Media 2070 is a project started by the Black Caucus at Free Press in 2020. Originally planned as a series of op-eds on media reparations, it evolved into an entire project due to the social upheaval of that year.

We often talk about making ‘the invisible media system’ more visible. Many Black people say they don't trust the media but might not be able to articulate why. Media 2070 began with a 100-page essay chronicling the history and legacy of the racist roots of today’s media system —from the Boston newsletter running runaway slave ads to the 2020 misinformation crisis.

The year 2070 reflects how in the roughly 50 years since the 1968 Kerner Commission report, major shifts have happened in the media system. We're asking: what would the media look like in another 50 years, in 2070, if it actually cherished Black stories? We're an afrofuturist project practicing Sankofa—going back to retrieve endangered stories in order to imagine new narratives that showcase our full humanity.

CCM: What does media reparations mean?

Tia Oso: The United Nations framework for reparations involves acknowledging systemic harm and recommending remedies that address past damage, while also preventing future harm. The idea of media reparations follows this model.

Our media system has deep roots in systemic racial harm. Colonial newspapers were funded by ads selling enslaved people. White-led newspapers participated in overthrowing democratically elected Black leaders in 1898 North Carolina, setting the stage for Jim Crow. Today's media fuels itself on crime beats and anti-Black tropes while enriching corporate power and white supremacy.

We need to stop media harm, address the ill-gotten gains of this system, and invest in Black communities’ ability to own and control our narratives from ideation through distribution. We need an abundant media system where we can tell our own stories and repair past harms. We see this work as part of the broader reparations movement. 

CCM: What is the role of Black community media in this work?

TO: Black media has always been part of the liberation movement, for nearly 200 years. The NY Amsterdam News is an example of a legacy Black community media organization at 115 years old. We see Black-owned community media being the recipients of a successful media reparations process. On that pathway, we believe the majority of nonprofit and public media funding should be going to Black-owned and BIPOC-owned media, community serving institutions. 

In our documentary "Black in the Newsroom," we asked Black people if they trust the media. Overwhelmingly, the answer was no, because of how anti-Blackness is embedded in the system. We've had to build our own information systems. With mainstream media being owned by billionaires and seemingly ruled by politics, Black-owned outlets like New York Amsterdam News may very well be positioned as the last defense for maintaining any semblance of democracy.

CCM: What is Black Narrative Power Month and how does it relate to the concept of abundance versus scarcity?

VW: Media reparations is realized through building Black narrative power—the ownership and control of our stories without interference. My background in ministry and organizing taught me the importance of stories: the story of us, the self within the whole, and how we overcome.

Black narrative power isn't just about bedtime stories but political education tools showing how things were done before and how we can move forward. Black Narrative Power Month commemorates Ida B. Wells—but not just journalists with formal training. The grandmother on her stoop sharing neighborhood happenings is also a trusted media-maker in her own right. 

As an afrofuturist project, we believe in abundance rather than scarcity. The myth that there isn't enough for everyone has caused communities and movements to fail. If we believe not only that there's enough, but that what's ours should be returned to us—that's where power and resistance emerge. We believe the future is beautifully abundant, but justice must prevail for us to see it.

CCM: What advice do you have for immigrant and in-language media on practicing reparative news?

TO: Every community needs to understand affirmative care practices. Our newsroom pledge addresses ways to confront harm and anti-Blackness. The idea that Black issues don't concern non-Black people is false and represents anti-Blackness itself.

As someone who organized in the migrant justice movement for years, I know anti-Blackness forms the root of America's racial hierarchy. Addressing it is everyone's responsibility because our research shows the media has harmed all non-white communities. Unfortunately, anti-Blackness often makes people think competitively about "my community" rather than seeing how our struggles connect. Building solidarity is essential.

CCM: Do you have any resources or additional information for our colleagues to read more about all this?

VW: The "Pledge to Care" is a good starting point for media outlets. "Black in the Newsroom" is another valuable resource—while it focuses on the Black experience, many will find elements that resonate with their own situations. I'm the child of an immigrant and have family members who don't speak English. Issues you might think don't affect Black people often do and vice versa.

TO: We're hosting a livestream panel about the legacy of Black storytelling on February 27 at 1pm EST to close out Black Narrative Power Month. We invite everyone to join the Media 2070 membership consortium to continue this work together.

Our 100-page essay documents media harms against Black communities while highlighting resistance throughout history. The Media 2070 toolkit provides practical steps for implementing reparative journalism practices.
