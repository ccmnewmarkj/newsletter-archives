Learning for THE CITY: On What Local Election Coverage Should Look Like

Community media journalists should prioritize audience engagement and hyperlocal focus over broad campaign coverage to maximize their impact during municipal election cycles, according to guidance from Rachel Holliday Smith, Managing Editor at THE CITY during a recent webinar hosted by CCM. 

Smith offered practical strategies for community-facing newsrooms, centered on leveraging limited resources by focusing intensively on issues that matter most to specific communities rather than attempting broad election coverage of mayoral and city council elections coming up in NYC this summer and fall.

"Get creative and just find the thing that is important to your community and really drill down on it and serve up what they need," Smith told participants. She emphasized that smaller outlets have an advantage in their ability to focus deeply on niche topics that larger publications might overlook or not have the expertise to cover.

Smith's recommendations included several key strategies for community media outlets:

  • Conduct audience surveys to identify priority issues and potential candidate questions before interviews
  • Focus resources on hyperlocal issues rather than attempting broad campaign coverage that does not differentiate your publication
  • Maintain transparency about editorial processes, especially regarding endorsements
  • Leverage audience input to generate story ideas and coverage angles

She stressed that direct audience engagement should be a cornerstone of election coverage strategy. "I love talking to our audience and asking them what they want to be covered. I find it really informs my coverage in a wonderful way. Your readers have a lot of ideas and a lot of questions that you may not think of."

Smith recommended implementing formal feedback mechanisms, suggesting community media publishers conduct a survey and ask “'What is important to you in this election? What do you want to know? And if we get a chance to talk to a candidate or two, what do you want to ask them?'"

Addressing concerns about editorial endorsements, Smith acknowledged the unique challenges facing community media publications where staff members often handle multiple roles since larger outlets can maintain separation between editorial boards and newsrooms. 

Smith suggested that endorsements can serve as valuable community service, particularly for immigrant populations. "They may not have the time to think about local politics. They're busy. They're maybe not as plugged in. So it's a service to endorse and it helps them make an informed decision.”.

For publications that do choose to endorse candidates, Smith emphasized the importance of transparency. "Explaining sort of behind the curtain how those decisions are made and how it will or will not affect coverage is a good idea” she advised.” Transparency is always better.”

Smith highlighted resource-sharing opportunities available to community outlets as well, noting that THE CITY allows free republication of its content. "We are free for all. We never have a paywall. It is part of our mission to get our stories in other places," she said, encouraging participants to republish relevant coverage rather than duplicating reporting efforts.

The session also reflected broader challenges facing community journalism, where resource constraints require strategic decision-making about coverage priorities. Smith's advice emphasized playing to smaller outlets' strengths rather than attempting to compete with larger publications on breadth of coverage.

Extra tip: Felix Perez of NY Archdiocese also addressed practical reporting challenges, including how to contact campaign staff for advertising and public relations purposes. He recommended working through campaign PR representatives, who are typically willing to facilitate connections with other campaign personnel.

More about Newmark J-School’s Report on the Advertising Boost Initiative 

In May, the Newmark J-School published a report examining the  the transformative impact of New York City’s legislation supporting community media outlets through advertising revenue as well as the impact of CCM’s Advertising Boost Initiative (ABI), led by Darlie Gervais. The report also recommends significant changes to bring more transparency to government spending and ensure editorial independence.

Lori Henson, manager of Government Advertising Policy at Rebuild Local News, continued the discussion, penning an op-ed for Poynter arguing that one way to save local news is to replicate the NYC law that earmarks a portion of government advertising budgets for community media outlets—despite the law's flaws.

Henson wrote: that “advertising procurement processes should protect institutional independence, structurally eliminating potential conflicts of interest. Those decisions should also be careful and intentional, with professional scrutiny of the best outlets to achieve marketing goals. When the same advertising goals can be met by either local news outlets or nonlocal media, the tie should go to local news for its many additional benefits to communities. The smallest news organizations need all the help they can get to compete for advertising with larger media outlets that have more resources.”
