Preserving history and using AI to document it

From Data to Story: Using AI for Reporting

CCM hosted a training session led by Joe Amditis from the Center for Cooperative Media at Montclair State University, focused on practical applications of AI tools for data journalism. The webinar demonstrated how journalists can use large language models (LLMs) like Gemini and Claude to transform unstructured data from PDFs and reports into structured, machine-readable formats like JSON and CSV.

Amditis emphasized that AI tools should augment, not replace, journalistic work. He showcased real-world examples, including extracting data from a New Jersey Comptroller's report on nursing home quality and converting it into interactive web dashboards. The session covered prompt engineering basics, explaining how to craft effective requests with clear instructions, context, and constraints.

He addressed ethical considerations and transparency in AI use. Amditis stressed that journalists must maintain the same verification standards for AI-generated content as any other source. He also introduced NotebookLM as a valuable tool for analyzing large documents while ensuring accuracy, as it only references uploaded source materials. The training concluded with practical advice for small newsrooms on implementing AI tools for repetitive tasks while maintaining journalistic integrity, including: 

  • Do the pre-work first: "The vast majority of value you get from these tools comes from gathering and verifying accurate information to give to these tools. Anytime you use them to generate new information, you're already messing up."
  • Focus on reformatting, not generating: "Use AI to reshuffle, reformat, repurpose or refactor information that you have already gathered, verified and authenticated" rather than creating new content from scratch.
  • Apply standard fact-checking practices: "Fact check AI output the same way you fact check anything - whether from a reporter, freelancer, or another organization. Nothing really changes here."
  • Start with repetitive tasks: Target "tasks that are repetitive and tedious... where I'm wearing out the paint on my control+C and control+V buttons" as ideal starting points for AI implementation in newsrooms.

Archiving AAPI History 

The Asian Media Initiative recently convened three leaders of community archives dedicated to preserving Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) history. The  webinar took placing  during this moment of renewed attacks on immigrant communities and efforts to erase diverse historical narratives from American education.

The discussion featured Randy Gonzalez of Filipino La., Naomi Oswald Kawamura from Densho, and Samip Mallick of the South Asian American Digital Archive (SAADA). Each panelist shared how their archives work to counter historical erasure and provide authentic community perspectives often missing from mainstream historical records.

Fighting Historical Erasure

Gonzalez explained how his archive work seeks to document Filipino American history has been systematically erased, despite Filipino Americans establishing the first permanent Asian American settlement at St. Malo, Louisiana in the 18th century. Gonzalez attributed this erasure to narrow educational narratives, assimilation pressures during segregation, and the suppression of stories about U.S. imperialism in the Philippines. "The archives allow us to kind of correct that and to put out things that belong to us that we see as valuable and we want to share," Gonzalez emphasized, noting how archives provide Filipino perspectives rather than westernized accounts of their experiences.

Kawamura discussed Densho's work documenting Japanese American incarceration during World War II through 1,300 video testimonies and 130,000 historical documents. She stressed that their oral histories capture the full human experience in the internment camps: "We don't curate or edit out aspects of joy that comes out. You know people were married in camp. They gave birth in camp." While preserving trauma narratives, Kawamura explained that "the [internment] incarceration doesn't define the community and we don't want to reduce Japanese Americans to a singular trauma, but it is a cautionary tale that has real relevance and resonance to the contemporary moment."

Mallick shared how SAADA’s work is not only to tell the story of immigration of South Asians to the U.S. but also address the vast diversity within South Asian American communities. Through the organization’s initiatives like the Archival Creators Fellowship SAADA endeavors to support historically marginalized voices such as those from queer, dalit, socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, and those outside of the dominant Indian American narrative, Mallick said. He added: “What community archives try to do…is to create a space to contest the historical erasures in the archive, but also they are really a result of our dedication, our love, and our belief in the importance of centering the voices of members of our community.” 

Tips for Starting or Maintaining a Digital Archive

  • Start simple and build gradually. 
  • Maintain technical control when possible. 
  • Record at highest quality and plan for technology changes. 
  • Let community members speak in their own voices.
  • Make archives active and relevant, not just storage. 
  • Collaborate and share resources.
