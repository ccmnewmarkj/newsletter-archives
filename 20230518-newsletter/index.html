Covering Hate Crimes in Black, Asian, Jewish, and LGBTQ Media
Join us on Friday, June 9. See more details and register here.
CCM is proud to host another cross-community event: a hybrid symposium on June 9 about how community media can and does respond to hate crimes targeting Black, Asian, Jewish, and LGBTQ communities. 

With the rise of anti-Asian American hate in the wake of the pandemic, a rise in anti-trans legislation, and mass shootings targeting Asian, Jewish, Black, LGBTQ+, and Latino communities, community media is more crucial than ever in reporting on this in a trauma-informed, ethical, safe, and effective way.

Co-led by our Asian Media Initiative Director Kavitha Rajagopalan and Black Media Initiative Director Cheryl Thompson-Morton, along with a panel of experts, we will attempt to answer a series of questions: 

  • How do community journalists help keep the most vulnerable members of their communities safe and informed, and bring diverse community members together to support each other? 
  • How can we cover hate crimes without re-traumatizing survivors? 
  • What is the legal and media history of how we define and document hate crimes?
  • How has data-driven hate crimes coverage functioned in our popular and in-community narratives around racism, solidarity, and safety?

As Thompson-Morton says: “This event aims to amplify the work being done within marginalized communities that are often ignored or misrepresented by mainstream narratives. We hope to foster solidarity and empathy among different groups that face discrimination and violence and empower our audiences with accurate and relevant information that can help them protect themselves and their rights.”

We are thrilled to have this event in person and streamed live, featuring experts Maya Wiley, president and CEO of The Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights and The Leadership Conference Education Fund, and Tamara K. Nopper, a sociologist, writer, educator, and editor - most recently of We Do This ‘Til We Free Us: Abolitionist Organizing and Transforming Justice (Haymarket Books, 2021), a book of Mariame Kaba’s writings and interviews.

The symposium will also include a panel discussion with Black, Asian, Jewish, and LGBTQ community media journalists covering hate crimes in their communities: Philissa Cramer of the Jewish Telegraphic Agency; Jason Villemez, Editor of the Philadelphia Gay News; Yiyan Zheng, a reporter at World Journal, the largest Chinese-language daily newspaper in the U.S.; and Christina Carrega, the National Criminal Justice Reporter for Capital B. 

The third component of our symposium are workshops to assist attendees with putting knowledge into practice and learn about editorial best practices, legal definitions and history of hate crimes, and analytic techniques. The two workshops offered will be led by Heather E. Murray, Managing Attorney of the Local Journalism Project at the Cornell Law School First Amendment Clinic, which offers pro bono legal support to journalists, advocating for public record access and First Amendment rights; and Michael Kilian, the New York state editor for Gannett, overseeing news sites upstate and downstate, including his hometown newspaper the Rochester Democrat and Chronicle, where he is the executive editor. 

“As we approach another fraught election season, disinformation campaigns will seek to pit vulnerable communities against each other as they have in recent national elections. It is critical that we build networks of information sharing and solidarity among our diverse community media as well, so that they might bring a broader context to their election coverage and defend their communities against damaging, divisive narratives,” Rajagopalan explains.

News from Inside the Community Media Sector

The Black Media Initiative is compiling a list of freelancers and experts interested in working with Black media. Know someone who might be interested? Share the links for folks to sign up.

Roy Wood Jr. roasts media scandals at White House Correspondents Dinner, but has a soft spot for local journalism.

Our friends at the Center for Cooperative Media at Montclair State University have come out with a report about the state of community and ethnic media in the state of New Jersey. From the report summary, we are able to see that “the number of ethnic and community news outlets in New Jersey increased by 15% since 2019.” There are currently more than 140 ethnic and community media outlets serving the Garden State in 15 languages. Check out the Events section below to register for a panel discussion about the report. 

The Daily Yonder has a piece on why readers of newspapers serving small communities are more open to new revenue-generating ideas than some publishers. The piece is based on a study titled ‘Revenue & Readership: Rescuing & Reviving Rural Journalism,’ done by researchers Teri Finneman, Associate Professor/Founder, University of Kansas/The Eudora Times; and Joey Young, Owner/Publisher, Kansas Publishing Ventures who spent 2022 collecting data from rural publishers and rural residents to create a proposed new model that is now in the experimental phase in Kansas. You can watch a recording of their presentation about the report. 

Macomb, Illinois was set to become a news desert until a news startup began publishing. WBEZ talks to the Community News Brief about harnessing existing journalism talent in central Illinois. 

The debate over ‘objectivity’ in journalism and the public’s trust in the media is still hotly contested. Columbia Journalism Review shares a piece on it by New York Times’ publisher A.G. Sulzberger on the inherent value of journalism. For more context, CJR also hosted a conference on the issues last year. 

The Los Angeles Times discusses whether charging Big Tech a “journalism usage fee” could help local news become more financially sustainable. 

After Vice News declared bankruptcy, the death of BuzzFeed News, and a series of layoffs across mainstream media outlets, what does all of it mean for the future of journalism? There may be some lessons to learn - and give - from this take in The Guardian. Here is some more information about the latest media bankruptcy.

The Philadelphia Inquirer was victim to a cyberattack and was unable to print its Sunday edition as a result. Staff and investigators are still trying to resume full operations and updates and figure out exactly what happened.

Congratulations are in order for the 27 newsrooms chosen to participate in The Poynter Institute’s 2023 Transforming Crime Reporting Into Public Safety Journalism program! Co-led by our own Black Media Initiative Director Cheryl Thompson-Morton, these newsrooms will work together for seven months to reimagine their approach to covering crime, courts and law enforcement and move towards creating more accurate and comprehensive community narratives about public safety.

A round of applause for Block Club Chicago! The outlet won five local journalism awards from  the Chicago Headline Club, the local branch of the Society of Professional Journalists, for a range of stories about issues including problems at pop-up COVID testing centers, traffic stops of Black drivers and the city’s most dangerous bike lane.

Nieman Lab takes a look at whether artificial intelligence could help local and community media outlets send newsletters to engage more readers. 

Also from Nieman Lab, the push to preserve ‘Black Twitter’ as the site loses some of its functionality and appeal. 

OP-ED: Poynter has published a piece by Kathleen McElroy, Director of the Journalism program at the University of Texas at Austin, about Black introspection and how 28 of the 29 Black people who have won individual Pulitzer Prizes in journalism through 2022 were awarded in categories usually not associated with hard news. 

Some insight from across the pond: Newsletter startup The Know discusses its large and growing subscriber base and high open rate as it combats news avoidance with solutions-based stories. Another story from the UK: as local news outlets shut down at an unprecedented rate there, local journalists have gone on strike to save community radio. 

American Press Institute is helping news organizations reimagine local opinion journalism to promote healthier civic discourse and to better understand its role in news business sustainability. This essay by Annafi Wahed, co-founder and CEO of The Flip Side, is part of a series of essays on the topic, and explores the fundraising and audience revenue opportunity local media have in spelling out an opinion-minded product’s goals.

This incarcerated journalist shows the need for a free press even in prisons, from Texas Observer. For more information on this issue, you can refer to the Prison Journalism Project and PEN America’s Prison and Justice Writing Program.

For our GenX and Xennial readers, a sad moment as MTV News is another victim in the slew of shutdowns in journalism. The New York Times explains how and why it had such an impact on news.

Fellowships & Opportunities

MuckRock is looking for a FOIA Fellow to make transparency their beat. The selected candidate will cover key developments in public records, helping respond to thorny question challenges while assisting our newsrooms around the country with our collaborative reporting efforts. Deadline is May 19. 

Applications open for the Isaac Rauch Fellowship at Documented. The NYC-based outlet is seeking an ambitious reporter for a six-month fellowship that seeks to uncover stories that matter to immigrant New Yorkers. Deadline is May 19. 

The 19th is recruiting for its 2023-2024 fellowship cohort of the Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Fellowship. Launched in partnership with Nikole Hannah-Jones, Howard University’s Knight chair in race and journalism, it will provide recent graduates, mid-career alums, and former students of Historically Black Colleges and Universities with full-year salaried and benefit-laden fellowships in the areas of reporting, editing, audience engagement and news product and technology. Deadline is May 31.

Open Campus is taking applications for the fall cohort of its HBCU Reporting Fellowship for currently enrolled students at Historically Black Colleges and Universities to report about news on their campuses. Deadline is June 1. 

The Peace Studio Fellowship is a year-long program advancing artists and journalists in becoming effective peacebuilders. This year, five artists and five journalists will be awarded a $10,000 grant to support socially transformative initiatives. Deadline is June 10.

Grist is accepting submissions for Imagine 2200: Write the future, its climate fiction short story contest. This is an invitation to writers from all over the globe to imagine a future in which solutions to the climate crisis flourish and help bring about radical improvements to our world. Deadline is June 13.

The Pulitzer Center, in collaboration with Diversify Photo, is taking applications for its Eyewitness Photojournalism Grant. This grant aims to tell visual stories about and by photojournalists from historically underrepresented communities. Deadline is June 30.

Another opportunity from Grist: the environmental news outlet and the Center for Rural Strategies launched the Rural Newswire, a website to support the rural U.S. news ecosystem last month. As part of this effort, both organizations will award $100,000 in reporting grants for stories that focus on and serve rural communities in the U.S. Deadline is July 1.
