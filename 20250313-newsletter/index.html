Harnessing the power of diversity, partnerships, and narrative for communities 

Community newsrooms from diverse backgrounds gathered at recent CCM forums to address critical industry challenges: sustainable funding models, equitable information access across languages, and the power of collaborative approaches to serving underrepresented communities. Through the Latino Media Initiative, Advertising Boost Initiative, and Black Media Initiative, leaders shared strategies for strengthening local journalism's impact during a time of unprecedented change.

These discussions revealed several shared priorities across community media outlets regardless of the specific populations they serve: the urgent need for sustainable business models that include both philanthropic support and strategic advertising partnerships; the importance of creating practical, accessible content that helps community members navigate daily challenges; and the value of preserving and building upon the historical legacy of community-focused journalism while embracing innovative approaches to narrative power and representation.

Latino Media Leaders Discuss Community Service Through Journalism

The Latino Media Initiative, led by Jesenia De Moya Correa, hosted a forum on the state of Latino media and how it is serving its communities during a time when immigrants and the media are facing unprecedented challenges. Speakers included Paola Jaramillo of Enlace Latino NC, Mónica Pirela of NotiVisión Georgia, and Macollvie Neel of The Haitian Times. 

Jaramillo shared how her nonprofit Spanish-language outlet in North Carolina focused on election coverage and hurricane response in 2024. "Latino communities in North Carolina face difficulties accessing information in Spanish, especially regarding government and legislative issues, and candidates do not effectively reach out to these communities," she explained. Jaramillo emphasized how her outlet focuses not just on journalism but also “on helping immigrants change their daily lives, rather than changing laws, which can take years, and focuses on helping the community integrate and navigate the system more easily."

Pirela discussed launching her digital news platform in 2018 after observing her family's struggle with information access. Both leaders highlighted funding challenges for Spanish-language media, with Jaramillo noting: "Latinos are very few in the world seeking funding from philanthropy, and it's necessary to educate philanthropists about why they deserve the same funding as those who produce English-language journalism."

Neel spoke about how The Haitian Times began 25 years ago when community media was just beginning to experience growth. “I think the past year has shown more than ever how critical publications like ours are in the changing media landscape and changing society,” Neel said. She also discussed how the publication’s definition, formalization, and strategy of community engagement has changed as the Haitian diasporic community has grown and changed over the years. This came to the forefront of Neel’s priorities during the 2024 elections when the publication decided to collaborate with groups already doing political work, host in-person events and conversations with Haitian communities all over the U.S., and provide more in-depth coverage in English. 

As De Moya Correa said, including Neal from The Haitian Times in a conversation about Latino media was essential because the current narrative is not inclusive enough of the experience of Black Caribbeans, Afro-Latinos, or Brazilians despite all of these immigrant communities facing the same socioeconomic issues and not being fully represented in mainstream media. “Having an outlet that has 25 years of experience serving diasporas on a transnational level, producing restorative and reparative journalism was vital,” she explained. 

“The context in which we are right now, which is a very trying, a very uncertain, very fast-paced, changing environment [including them] not only was an act of justice, but also an act of inclusion that provided space for everyone within the Latino landscape,” De Moya Correa added.

Securing Advertising for Sustainability 

Unlocking advertising revenue—a critical pillar of sustainability for community media—was the focus of a discussion about forming strategic partnerships with local businesses, led by CCM Advertising Boost Initiative Director Darlie Gervais. The session explored concrete pathways for news organizations to build mutually beneficial partnerships with local chambers of commerce and small businesses, potentially transforming how community outlets approach revenue generation. Dina Rabiner, Vice President of Economic Development and Strategic Partnerships at the Brooklyn Chamber of Commerce, shared insights on how media organizations can leverage chamber resources while simultaneously expanding chambers' reach to diverse audiences.The Brooklyn Chamber is the largest Chamber of Commerce in the city and the state and Rabiner highlighted the services it provides to borough businesses including legal assistance, business courses, state and federal government lobbying opportunities, recruitment services, and neighborhood economic development advising. The Chamber also offers financing through Brooklyn Alliance Capital, primarily serving minority- and immigrant-owned businesses.

Rabiner emphasized the mutual benefits of media-Chamber partnerships to the publishers in attendance. "You also have a lot to offer a chamber because you have a wide audience that might not be reached by the chamber." She also explored various events and opportunities the Chamber can host for publishers to meet business owners in person. 

Zachary Richner who is the founding member of the Empire State Local News Coalition, a group of 200 New York news organizations, was also present at the event and gave a brief talk about the Lift Our Communities, Advertise Locally (LOCAL) Act. If passed, the proposal would ensure minority-, woman- or veteran-owned businesses, as well as any business with 10 employees or fewer, would be able to receive a refundable tax credit of 80% of its first $5,000 of local advertising for a maximum credit of $4,000 per year. 

Richner said supporting the push for this legislation is a chance to show that “these two legs of the stool work together: the small business community and the chambers and the local news.” 

The conversation could serve as a blueprint for community media publishers to contact their local Chambers of Commerce and build strategic partnerships with area businesses. You can watch the whole event here. 

The Future of Black Community Media

As Black media approaches its bicentennial milestone, CCM's Black Media Futures conference explored both its historical foundations and innovative future. Moderated by Black Media Initiative Director Cheryl Thompson-Morton, the conference examined three critical dimensions of Black journalism: its ongoing political significance, the urgent need to preserve its historical record, and strategies for building narrative power in an evolving media landscape. White House correspondent April Ryan of Black Press USA delivered a keynote that connected today's challenges to the Black press's enduring mission of speaking truth to power.

Thompson-Morton emphasized the historical importance of Black media: "Nearly 200 years later, Black media continues to create a space where Black folks can speak for ourselves about issues of importance and combat the stereotypes that harm us."

Ryan offered a firsthand account of the challenges facing Black journalists in Washington: "Covering this White House, period, is a hard thing. But when you happen to be Black and a woman and not part of that white male dominated fraternity in Washington, especially in this moment, it's even harder." She characterized the Black press as being "at a crossroads" amid significant economic shifts affecting Black communities. Ryan highlighted how outlets like hers are uniquely positioned to cover stories overlooked by mainstream media, pointing specifically to the economic impact of federal workforce reductions on Washington DC's substantial Black middle class—a critical story she noted is largely absent from broader coverage despite its significant implications.

Ryan was followed by sessions on archiving Black media and building Black narrative power with the staff of Free Press’ Media 2070 initiative. Dr. Allissa V. Richardson, an associate professor of journalism at the University of Southern California and founder of the Charlotta Bass Journalism and Justice Lab which trains students in social justice storytelling, spoke about her research into how Black journalists and community members use mobile and social media to produce innovative forms of journalism to document Black Lives Matter, and building what she called the “fugitive Black archive.” Brandon Nightingale, senior project manager of Howard University's Black Press Archives, explained the breadth and depth of digitizing and organizing historical Black newspapers and the importance of collaboration with community groups to record and access the information. 

Media 2070’s Tia Oso, Diamond Hardiman, and Venneikia Williams led the third session on building Black narrative power, offering historical context on media oppression and resistance while discussing media reparations as a path toward repair and justice for Black communities. For those who have been following our newsletter, we spoke with Oso and Williams last month about community media and reparative journalism. 

The half day conference was insightful and involved much more information on archiving, monetizing historical records, and how to practice reparative journalism. You can watch the entire event here.
