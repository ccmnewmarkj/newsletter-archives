 Balancing safety and editorial responsibility: How Conecta Arizona’s Maritza Félix navigates the challenges

Maritza Félix is the director and founder of Conecta Arizona, a Spanish-language news site that connects communities in the U.S.-Mexico border region. She used to worry about the deadlines and funding, and whether or not her reporters were wearing sunscreen. However, this has changed with  the sharp rise in political rhetoric around immigration and law enforcement crackdown on the non-citizen journalists and immigrant communities  since the beginning of President Donald Trump’s second term. Now, she and her staff grapple with threats to their safety and a daily concern about whether practicing lawful journalism could jeopardize their ability to remain in the country. 

This came to a head for Félix last month, when conservative media personality Charlie Kirk was killed in Utah and the memorial service was set for Glendale, Arizona. It was a huge breaking news story in Conecta’s coverage area – and she was forced to make very hard decisions about how her team would respond. 

CCM talked to her about her coverage choices, navigating reporting with a staff that has mixed immigration status, and her advice for other community media publishers in our network who may be in a similar position. 

This Q&A was edited for clarity. 

CCM: Can you walk us through what was going on in your newsroom the day of Kirk’s death? How did your readers react and did that impact your coverage? 

MFélix: When the shooting happened, I was on a panel at the Online News Association conference in New Orleans talking about the work that we do as immigrant community reporters, and at the same time, [my team was] hosting our newsroom’s daily cafecito - or coffee time - on WhatsApp. It’s about 400 of our readers and community members who get together every afternoon and we just talk about whatever is going on. We can talk about news, we can talk about food, we can talk about whatever.

So, I was feeling my cell phone in my pocket ringing and vibrating a lot. When the panel was done, I looked at my phone, and then I started seeing everything that happened and the opinions that we had in our group that were completely different. Some of them were saying he was a very controversial person, he deserved it. And then the other person was saying no, we shouldn't be celebrating this. 

So when I was reading the cafecito chat after the panel, I realized that that's a topic that was important for my community. Even though not everybody related to it or knew who he was, his death and the reaction of this country to his death was worth talking about and exploring.

CCM: Did you explore it with your readers in the chat itself? 

MFélix: Yes, we did something very basic with laying out the facts we knew. Then since our staff has done political violence-related training on how to de-escalate the polarization on those topics, we were trying to put those tactics into practice that day and in the days that followed in the cafecito chats.

Then they announced the funeral. The funeral was going to be huge; it was in the same stadium as the Super Bowl. So I had a meeting with our art director, who's our photographer and social media community manager, and our managing editor and I told them I think it's very important that we can cover this, but how do we do it safely? We were concerned because of the status of my team – there are green card holders, some of them are on work visas. [We realized that] sending them to cover an event with so many people who have spoken out so loudly against immigration and our community was going to be impossible to do safely.

CCM: You still managed to publish some coverage for the benefit of your readers, even if it wasn’t your usual reporting and editorial routine. Can you take us through that process? 

MFélix: We leaned on and are grateful for our partnership with the Associated Press. They provide a lot of their content for free for very local, small community organizations. And I asked them, do you know if everything available for republication will be in Spanish? Can we use AP photos? [Editor’s note: This content sharing service is part of a pilot program run by the AP Fund for Journalism.]

So having the support and knowing that the AP has our back, knowing that they're going to be covering [it] and regardless we're going to have the information without putting our team at risk – that was very important. 

One of our freelancers did go to the event and did a couple of interviews outside, but she made sure to follow the protocol of hostile environment training and we used some of her videos – posting without any bylines from our staff so no one was put at risk. I do feel bad for not giving the reporters bylines on their work. Sometimes we do use AP or just write ‘Staff’ for the byline. They don't see their name, and they feel a little bit discouraged since they worked so hard. But, I tell them this is for your safety and hopefully in three years we can go back and just change those bylines and you'll know that that's yours. Or, we can put a note on it and send it for an award or something. But right now, this is what we need to do to keep you safe.

CCM: Do you see yourself doing this going forward on more stories?

MFélix: Yes. And the thing is, at the beginning of the year, we were thinking that things were not going to get that bad. But everything has been worse. Before I would worry but it was basically: wear sunscreen, take an extra battery for your phone, and make your deadline. Now, some of our reporters have been victims of harassment in airports, at the border, at events, and online. We cannot normalize that, and we cannot diminish any potential threats that we have. 

We have people who help us identify threats like PEN America, the International Women's Media Foundation, and Access Now. 

We're also very conscious of who's going with who to cover what kind of story, what part of the cities are more friendly to us, who is going to be carrying their documents, etc. 

CCM: What’s your advice for other community-facing media that might be facing the same challenges and who may be worried about being journalists right now? 

MFélix: I know sometimes being outspoken on things that are very controversial is hard and scary. But, I don't think this is that moment to be quiet. I think this is a moment of action, and we should embrace our identity. 

You can also work with freelancers. They don't need to be on your payroll so you can assign stories that you don't want your team for whatever reason to write about.

Also, ask for help! 

We're very, very lucky to have the AP support. We cannot afford to pay for AP services because it is really expensive. But, this pilot program has been so good for us. We sometimes get denied credentials because we're too small or too community-focused or too Latino, or whatever. But, they always get access to those things and have a lot of support and resources, so we make sure to take advantage of it.

Look for those kinds of opportunities. There is a lot of support because now, after a long time, there's a lot of people who are opening their eyes to our realities as immigrants and immigrant journalists.
